\begin{learningobjectives}
After working through this chapter you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item choose a missing-data strategy from the \emph{mechanism}, not from
        convenience, and say what each one biases;
  \item standardise and normalise a small dataset by hand, and state which
        models require it;
  \item explain why fitting a scaler before splitting is a leak, with the
        arithmetic that shows it;
  \item compute a correlation and demonstrate that it can be zero for perfectly
        dependent variables;
  \item run PCA on a $2\times2$ covariance matrix by hand;
  \item name the drift type behind a production degradation and the monitor
        that would detect it.
\end{itemize}
\end{learningobjectives}

\begin{plainlanguage}
Most of the work in a machine-learning project happens before any model exists,
and most of the damage happens there too. Data arrives with gaps, duplicates,
inconsistent units, and quiet encoding decisions made by whoever built the
system that produced it. None of that shows up as an error later --- it shows up
as a model that works in testing and disappoints in use.
\end{plainlanguage}

\begin{engineernote}
Every transform in this chapter is a function fitted on data, and the rule for
all of them is the same: \emph{fit on training data only, apply everywhere}.
That applies to scalers, imputers, encoders, vocabularies, PCA bases, and
feature selectors alike. The moment a transform sees the validation or test set
before it is scored, the score stops meaning what you think it means.
\end{engineernote}

\section{Data Preprocessing}

% ---- 4.1.1 Data Cleaning ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data Cleaning}

\begin{cautionbox}[Missingness has a mechanism, and it decides the fix]
Before choosing how to handle a gap, ask why it is there.
\begin{description}
  \item[MCAR --- missing completely at random] The gap is unrelated to
    anything. Dropping rows is unbiased, just wasteful. This is rare.
  \item[MAR --- missing at random] The gap depends on other \emph{observed}
    variables: older patients skip a questionnaire item. Imputation
    conditioned on those variables is valid.
  \item[MNAR --- missing not at random] The gap depends on the \emph{unobserved
    value itself}: high earners decline to state income; a failing sensor stops
    reporting. No imputation can fix this, because the data needed to correct
    it is exactly the data that is absent.
\end{description}
The dangerous case is the third, because it looks identical to the first two in
the file. Mean imputation under MNAR does not merely add noise --- it
systematically pulls the missing group toward the population average and erases
the very signal you were trying to model. Always add a binary
\texttt{was\_missing} indicator: whether a value was absent is often more
predictive than the value would have been.
\end{cautionbox}

\begin{v3topic}{Why Data Cleaning?}
    Real-world data is rarely clean: values may be missing, erroneous, duplicated, or inconsistent.
    Data cleaning (also \emph{data scrubbing}) identifies and corrects these issues before any analysis\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
    Garbage in, garbage out: dirty data systematically corrupts model outputs.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Incompleteness}: missing attributes or records
        \item \textbf{Noise}: random error or variance in values
        \item \textbf{Inconsistency}: conflicting codes or names
        \item \textbf{Duplicates}: same entity recorded multiple times
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Missing Data: MCAR, MAR, MNAR}
    Rubin (1976) classified missingness into three mechanisms\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}:
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,2cm]|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
        MCAR & Missing Completely At Random — missingness is independent of all data \\
        MAR  & Missing At Random — missingness depends on observed data only \\
        MNAR & Missing Not At Random — missingness depends on the unobserved value itself \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{The three missingness mechanisms. Only the mechanism determines whether imputation is valid, and the file itself never tells you which one you have.}
\label{tab:data-missingness}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Outlier Detection}
    Outliers are observations that deviate markedly from the overall pattern\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
    Two classical rules for univariate detection:
\visualseparator
    \textbf{IQR rule}: flag $x$ if $x < Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$ or $x > Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$, where $\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$.

    \textbf{Z-score rule}: flag $x$ if $|z| > 3$, where
    \begin{equation}
        z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
    \end{equation}
\where{$x$ is the raw value, $\mu$ the mean and $\sigma$ the standard
deviation of the feature; $|z|>3$ is a common, and arbitrary, outlier flag.}
    Multivariate outliers require distance-based methods (Mahalanobis) or density-based methods (LOF).

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Imputation Strategies}
    Imputation replaces missing values with plausible substitutes rather than discarding whole records\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Notes} \\
        Mean / median      & Simple; distorts variance \\
        Mode (categorical) & Assigns most frequent class \\
        Forward/back fill  & Time series; propagates last known value \\
        k-NN imputation    & Uses similar records; better for MAR \\
        Model-based (MICE) & Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations; commonly relies on a Missing At Random assumption, while MNAR requires explicit sensitivity or missingness modelling \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Imputation strategies and what each assumes. All of them reduce variance and none of them adds information --- always keep a was-missing indicator.}
\label{tab:data-imputation}

\begin{workedexample}[What mean imputation actually does to your data]
A feature with two missing values:
$[\,10,\ 12,\ \textsf{NA},\ 14,\ \textsf{NA},\ 200\,]$.

\emph{Compute the substitutes} from the four observed values:
\[
  \text{mean} = \frac{10+12+14+200}{4} = 59,
  \qquad
  \text{median} = \frac{12+14}{2} = 13 .
\]

\emph{Mean imputation} gives $[10, 12, \mathbf{59}, 14, \mathbf{59}, 200]$.
The two invented values are larger than three of the four real ones, because
the single outlier $200$ dragged the mean up. Median imputation gives
$[10, 12, \mathbf{13}, 14, \mathbf{13}, 200]$, which is far more plausible.

\emph{Now the damage.} Population variance over the four observed values is
\[
  \frac{49^2+47^2+45^2+141^2}{4} = \frac{26{,}516}{4} = 6{,}629,
  \qquad s = 81.4 .
\]
After mean imputation the deviations of the two new values are exactly zero,
so the numerator is unchanged while the count rises to six:
\[
  \frac{26{,}516}{6} = 4{,}419,\qquad s = 66.5 .
\]
Variance fell by a third without a single new observation. In general,
imputing $k$ values at the mean multiplies the variance by $n/(n+k)$ --- the
more you impute, the more confident your data falsely looks.

\emph{The consequence.} Every downstream standard error, confidence interval,
and significance test is now too narrow. This is why the honest minimum is to
add a \texttt{was\_missing} indicator column: it lets the model use the fact of
absence, and it lets you find out later how much of your result rests on
invented numbers.
\end{workedexample}
\end{v3topic}

\begin{v3topic}{Data Fusion}
    Data fusion combines information from heterogeneous sources to produce a more complete or accurate dataset than any single source provides alone\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
    Challenges include conflicting values (\emph{data conflicts}), different granularities (\emph{data aggregation mismatch}), and temporal drift (sources updated at different frequencies).
    Resolution strategies range from source trust ranking and majority voting to probabilistic graphical models that jointly estimate the true value.
\end{v3topic}

\subsection{Data Transformation}

\begin{workedexample}[Standardise and normalise the same five numbers]
Take $x = (2,\,4,\,4,\,4,\,6)$.

\emph{Mean and standard deviation.}
$\mu = \frac{2+4+4+4+6}{5} = 4$;
deviations $(-2,0,0,0,2)$, squares $(4,0,0,0,4)$, so
$\sigma^2 = 8/5 = 1.6$ and $\sigma = 1.265$.

\emph{Standardisation} $z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$:
\[
  z = (-1.581,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 1.581),
  \qquad \text{mean } 0,\ \text{standard deviation } 1 .
\]

\emph{Min--max normalisation} to $[0,1]$, with $\min = 2$ and $\max = 6$:
\[
  x' = \frac{x-2}{6-2} = (0,\ 0.5,\ 0.5,\ 0.5,\ 1) .
\]

\emph{Which to use.} Standardisation keeps outliers visible and is what
distance- and gradient-based methods want (SVM, $k$-NN, $k$-means, PCA, neural
networks). Min--max forces a fixed range and is what you want when a bounded
input is required --- but a single extreme value compresses everything else
into a narrow band, so it is fragile precisely when the data is dirty.
Tree-based models need neither.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}[Why fitting the scaler before splitting is a leak]
Suppose the training rows have mean $10$ and the test rows, drawn from a later
period, have mean $30$.

\emph{Correct.} Fit on training only: $\mu = 10$. Test values are standardised
using $\mu = 10$, so their standardised mean comes out near $+2$ --- and the
model, quite correctly, sees that the test period is unusual.

\emph{Leaked.} Fit on everything: $\mu = 20$. Now training data centres at
$-1$ and test at $+1$, and the shift between the two periods has been
\emph{partially removed by the preprocessing itself}. Validation accuracy
improves. Deployment accuracy does not, because in production there is no
future data to average with.

The leak is small, silent, and flatters the model --- the three properties that
make a measurement error dangerous.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{v3topic}{Min-Max Normalization}
    Rescales values to a fixed interval $[a, b]$, typically $[0, 1]$\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
    Preserves the original distribution shape but is sensitive to outliers.
    \begin{equation}
        x' = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}(b - a) + a
    \end{equation}
\where{$x_{\min},x_{\max}$ are the observed extremes of the feature and
$[a,b]$ the target range (usually $[0,1]$). Both extremes must come from the
training split only.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Z-Score Standardization}
    Centers and scales to zero mean and unit variance\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
    Preferred when the distribution is approximately normal or when the algorithm is sensitive to scale (SVMs, PCA, gradient descent).
    \begin{equation}
        x' = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\mu$ and $\sigma$ are estimated on the training data alone; reusing
them unchanged at inference is what keeps the transform honest.}
    After standardization: $\mathbb{E}[x'] = 0$, $\mathrm{Var}(x') = 1$.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Categorical Encoding}
    Algorithms typically require numerical input; categorical variables must be encoded\textsuperscript{\cite{molinHandsonDataAnalysis2021}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Encoding}    & \textbf{When to use} \\
        One-hot (dummy)       & Nominal; no ordinal relationship; creates $k$ binary columns \\
        Label encoding        & Ordinal or tree-based models where ordering is irrelevant \\
        Target encoding       & High-cardinality nominals; replaces category with mean target value \\
        Binary / Hash trick   & Very high cardinality; reduces dimensionality at cost of collisions \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Encoding schemes for categorical variables. One-hot is safe but explodes with cardinality; target encoding is compact and leaks unless fitted inside the cross-validation fold.}
\label{tab:data-encoding}

\begin{workedexample}[All four encodings applied to the same column]
A \textsf{colour} column with three categories, and a binary target:

\smallskip
\begin{tblr}{
  width=0.5\linewidth,
  colspec={Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]},
  row{1}={font=\bfseries,bg=AlmanachMist},
  hlines,vlines}
row & colour & converted \\
1 & red & 1 \\
2 & green & 0 \\
3 & blue & 1 \\
4 & red & 0 \\
5 & red & 1 \\
6 & green & 0 \\
\end{tblr}
\smallskip

\emph{One-hot.} One new binary column per category; exactly one is $1$ per
row. Three categories become three columns:

\smallskip
\begin{tblr}{
  width=0.62\linewidth,
  colspec={Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]},
  row{1}={font=\bfseries,bg=AlmanachMist},
  hlines,vlines}
row & is\_red & is\_green & is\_blue \\
1 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
2 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
3 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{tblr}
\smallskip

No ordering is implied, which is the point. The cost is one column per
category --- a postcode field with $8{,}000$ values becomes $8{,}000$ columns.

\emph{Label encoding.} Map each category to an integer:
$\textsf{blue}\to0$, $\textsf{green}\to1$, $\textsf{red}\to2$. Row~1 becomes
$2$, row~2 becomes $1$, row~3 becomes $0$.

\textbf{This asserts that $\textsf{blue}<\textsf{green}<\textsf{red}$ and that
green is exactly halfway between.} A linear model, a distance metric, or a
neural network will use that ordering, and it is fiction. Safe only for
genuinely ordinal data (\textsf{small}/\textsf{medium}/\textsf{large}) or for
tree models, which only ever split on ``$\leq$ some threshold'' and so are
insensitive to which integers were chosen.

\emph{Target encoding.} Replace each category by the mean target of its rows:
\[
  \textsf{red} = \tfrac{1+0+1}{3} = 0.667,\qquad
  \textsf{green} = \tfrac{0+0}{2} = 0.000,\qquad
  \textsf{blue} = \tfrac{1}{1} = 1.000 .
\]
One column instead of three, and it carries real signal.

\textbf{But look at \textsf{blue}.} Its value of $1.000$ comes from a single
row --- and that row's own target is inside its encoding. The model can read
the answer off the feature. This is target leakage, and it is why target
encoding must be fitted \emph{inside} each cross-validation fold, using only
the other folds' rows.

\emph{Smoothed target encoding} damps the small-category problem by pulling
each estimate toward the overall mean $\bar y = 3/6 = 0.5$:
\[
  \text{enc}(c) = \frac{\text{sum}_c + m\bar y}{n_c + m},
  \qquad\text{with } m = 2:
\]
\[
  \textsf{red} = \frac{2 + 1}{3+2} = 0.600,\qquad
  \textsf{green} = \frac{0 + 1}{2+2} = 0.250,\qquad
  \textsf{blue} = \frac{1 + 1}{1+2} = 0.667 .
\]
\textsf{Blue} has fallen from a confident $1.000$ to a cautious $0.667$,
because one observation is not evidence.
\where{$n_c$ is the number of rows in category $c$, $\text{sum}_c$ their target
total, $\bar y$ the overall mean, and $m>0$ the smoothing weight --- effectively
``how many imaginary average rows to add''. Larger $m$ trusts small categories
less.}

\emph{Hashing trick.} Apply a hash function and take the remainder:
$\text{col} = h(c) \bmod k$. With $k=2$, two of the three colours land in the
same column and become indistinguishable. That collision is accepted
deliberately: the feature count is fixed at $k$ regardless of how many
categories appear, including ones never seen in training. Use it when
cardinality is unbounded --- URLs, user agents, free-text tags --- and you can
tolerate the collisions.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{cautionbox}[Unseen categories at inference time]
Every scheme above needs an answer for a category that appears in production
but not in training. One-hot must reserve an explicit \textsf{other} column;
label and target encoding need a defined fallback (the overall mean is the
usual choice for target encoding); only the hashing trick handles it without
any special case, because an unseen value simply hashes somewhere.

If you have not decided what happens for an unseen category, your pipeline has
already decided --- usually by crashing, or worse, by silently encoding it as
zero and thereby claiming it is the first category.
\end{cautionbox}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Log and Power Transforms}
    Highly skewed features violate the normality assumptions of many models and can dominate distance metrics\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\log(x + 1)$: compresses right-skewed data (counts, prices)
        \item Square-root: moderate skew, preserves zeros
        \item Box--Cox: parametric family $x^{(\lambda)} = \frac{x^\lambda - 1}{\lambda}$; $\lambda$ estimated from data
        \item Yeo--Johnson: extends Box--Cox to negative values
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

\subsection{Data Reduction}

\begin{v3topic}{Feature Selection vs. Feature Extraction}
    Both reduce dimensionality, but differ in approach\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Feature Selection} & \textbf{Feature Extraction} \\
        Selects a subset of original features & Creates new features from originals \\
        Preserves interpretability & May lose interpretability \\
        Filter, Wrapper, Embedded methods & PCA, autoencoders, LDA \\
        No transformation of values & Projects data into new space \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Selection keeps original columns and stays interpretable; extraction builds new combinations and usually does not.}
\label{tab:data-selection-extraction}

\begin{workedexample}[The same problem, solved both ways]
Five features predicting loan default: \textsf{income}, \textsf{savings},
\textsf{debt}, \textsf{age}, \textsf{postcode}.

\emph{Selection} keeps a subset. Suppose a filter method ranks features by
correlation with the target and drops the weakest two, leaving
\textsf{income}, \textsf{savings}, \textsf{debt}. The surviving columns are
untouched, so a decision can still be explained as ``declined because debt is
high relative to income'' --- and \cref{ch:it-law} requires exactly that kind
of explanation.

\emph{Extraction} builds new features. PCA on the same five columns might
produce a first component
\[
  z_1 = 0.62\,\textsf{income} + 0.55\,\textsf{savings}
        - 0.51\,\textsf{debt} + 0.21\,\textsf{age} + 0.04\,\textsf{postcode},
\]
which captures more variance in one column than any single original feature.
But $z_1$ is not ``income'' --- it is a blend that has no name, no unit, and no
meaning to an applicant.

\emph{The trade, stated plainly.} Extraction usually compresses better;
selection stays explainable. Two practical notes: filter methods rank features
independently and so cannot see that two useless features are predictive
\emph{together}, while wrapper methods evaluate subsets against an actual model
and are correspondingly slower; and both must be fitted inside the
cross-validation fold, because selecting features using the full dataset leaks
the test set just as surely as fitting a scaler on it does.
\end{workedexample}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{PCA: Principal Component Analysis}
    PCA finds orthogonal directions (principal components) of maximum variance in the data\textsuperscript{\cite{hastieElementsStatisticalLearning2008}}.
    Components are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$, ordered by descending eigenvalue.
    Projecting onto the top-$k$ components minimizes reconstruction error under the Frobenius norm.
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_k, \quad \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\mathbf{X}$ is the $n\times d$ data matrix, $\mathbf{W}_k$ the
matrix whose columns are the top $k$ eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and
$\mathbf{Z}$ the $n\times k$ projected data.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Sampling Strategies}
    When full-data computation is infeasible, sampling produces a representative subset\textsuperscript{\cite{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Method}           & \textbf{How}                                    & \textbf{Use case} \\
        Simple random             & Each record drawn with equal probability         & General baseline \\
        Stratified                & Draw proportionally from each stratum            & Imbalanced classes \\
        Cluster                   & Select clusters, then sample within              & Geographically distributed data \\
        Systematic                & Every $k$-th record                              & Ordered datasets \\
        Reservoir (online)        & Fixed-size sample from a stream in one pass      & Streaming / big data \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Sampling strategies. Stratification matters most exactly when the class of interest is rare, which is when a random split is least reliable.}
\label{tab:data-sampling}

\begin{workedexample}[Why stratification matters most when it matters most]
A dataset of $100{,}000$ rows with $1\,\%$ positives ($1{,}000$ of them). You
need a sample of $1{,}000$ rows.

\emph{Simple random sampling.} The expected number of positives is
$1{,}000 \times 0.01 = 10$. But the count is binomial, with standard deviation
\[
  \sqrt{np(1-p)} = \sqrt{1000 \times 0.01 \times 0.99} = \sqrt{9.9} = 3.15 .
\]
A $95\,\%$ range is therefore roughly $10 \pm 6$, i.e.\ \textbf{4 to 16
positives}. On an unlucky draw you have four examples of the thing you are
trying to model --- and you will not know, because the sample looks fine.

\emph{Stratified sampling.} Draw $1\,\%$ from each class separately: exactly
$10$ positives and $990$ negatives, every single time. The variance in the
positive count is zero, not small.

\emph{Read the general rule.} The relative error of the rare-class count in a
random sample scales as $1/\sqrt{np}$, so it grows precisely as the class gets
rarer. Stratification costs one extra line of code and removes that failure
entirely --- which is why the same argument reappears for train/test splitting
in \cref{ch:machine-learning}.

\emph{One caution.} If you go further and \emph{oversample} the rare class to
balance it, you have changed the base rate the model sees, so its predicted
probabilities no longer match reality and the decision threshold must be
recalibrated.
\end{workedexample}
\end{v3topic}

\subsection{Foundations of Data Systems}

\subsubsection{Reliability}

\begin{v3topic}{Reliability in Data Systems}
    A reliable system continues to work correctly even when faults occur\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    A \emph{fault} (one component misbehaves) must be distinguished from a \emph{failure} (the whole system stops delivering its service).
    Systems are made fault-tolerant by tolerating faults before they escalate to failures.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Hardware faults}: RAID disks, dual power, hot standby
        \item \textbf{Software faults}: extensive testing, process isolation, chaos engineering
        \item \textbf{Human faults}: administrative interfaces with guard rails, easy rollback, monitoring
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{MTBF, MTTR, Availability}
    Reliability is quantified using failure-rate metrics\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Availability} = \frac{\text{MTBF}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}}
    \end{equation}
\where{MTBF is the mean time between failures and MTTR the mean time to
repair; halving repair time raises availability exactly as much as doubling
time between failures.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{MTBF} — Mean Time Between Failures
        \item \textbf{MTTR} — Mean Time To Recovery
        \item \textbf{SLA} — Service Level Agreement: contractual availability target (e.g., 99.9\% = 8.7 h downtime/year)
        \item \textbf{Redundancy}: $n+1$ or $2n$ hot standbys eliminate single points of failure
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Scalability}

\begin{v3topic}{Vertical vs. Horizontal Scaling}
    As data volume grows, systems must scale\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Aspect}    & \textbf{Vertical (Scale Up)} & \textbf{Horizontal (Scale Out)} \\
        Approach   & Bigger machine (more CPU/RAM) & More machines in a cluster \\
        Limit      & Single-node hardware ceiling  & Near-linear with node count \\
        Cost model & Non-linear (premium hardware) & Commodity servers \\
        Failure    & Single point of failure       & Graceful degradation \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Scaling up against scaling out. Vertical scaling is simpler and has a ceiling; horizontal scaling has no ceiling and imports distributed-systems problems.}
\label{tab:data-scaling}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{CAP Theorem}
    In a distributed system it is impossible to simultaneously guarantee all three of\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Consistency} (C): every read returns the most recent write
        \item \textbf{Availability} (A): every request receives a (non-error) response
        \item \textbf{Partition tolerance} (P): the system operates despite network partitions
    \end{itemize}
    Since partitions are unavoidable in real networks, designers must choose between CP and AP systems.
    E.g., HBase (CP), Cassandra (AP), RDBMS in single node (CA).

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Load Balancing}
    Load balancing distributes incoming requests across multiple servers to prevent any single node from becoming a bottleneck\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    Common algorithms: round-robin, least-connections, IP-hash, weighted round-robin.
    A \emph{load balancer} also provides health checks; unhealthy nodes are removed from the pool automatically.
    For data-intensive systems, \emph{data partitioning} (sharding) complements load balancing by splitting the data itself — e.g., consistent hashing ensures minimal data migration when nodes are added or removed.
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Maintainability}

\begin{v3topic}{Three Pillars of Maintainability}
    Kleppmann (2017) identifies three design goals that make systems maintainable over time\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,2.5cm]|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Property}   & \textbf{Meaning}                                                  & \textbf{Practices} \\
        Operability          & Easy for ops teams to keep running day-to-day                     & Good monitoring, documentation, predictable behavior \\
        Simplicity           & New engineers can understand the system                           & Abstraction, clean APIs, remove accidental complexity \\
        Evolvability         & Easy to adapt to new requirements                                 & Modular design, backward-compatible schemas, CI/CD \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{The maintainability properties a data system is judged on long after the first version ships.}
\label{tab:data-maintainability}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 4.1.6 Data Processing at Scale -----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data Processing at Scale}

\subsubsection{Batch Processing}

\begin{v3topic}{MapReduce Paradigm}
    MapReduce (Dean \& Ghemawat, 2004) is a programming model for processing large datasets in parallel across a cluster\textsuperscript{\cite{leskovecMiningMassiveDatasets2014}}.
    The programmer provides two functions; the framework handles data distribution, fault recovery, and aggregation automatically.
\visualseparator
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Map}: each worker applies a function to its input split and emits $(key, value)$ pairs
        \item \textbf{Shuffle \& Sort}: framework groups all values by key across all workers
        \item \textbf{Reduce}: each worker aggregates values for its assigned keys into a final result
    \end{enumerate}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Hadoop \& Apache Spark}
    Hadoop provides a distributed file system (HDFS) and a MapReduce runtime\textsuperscript{\cite{leskovecMiningMassiveDatasets2014}}.
    Apache Spark generalizes MapReduce with in-memory computation on Resilient Distributed Datasets (RDDs), delivering 10--100$\times$ speedups for iterative algorithms.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Feature}       & \textbf{Hadoop MR}     & \textbf{Spark} \\
        Computation model      & Disk-based             & In-memory (RDD/DataFrame) \\
        Latency                & High (minutes)         & Low (seconds) \\
        Iterative algorithms   & Painful                & Efficient \\
        Ecosystem              & HDFS, YARN, Hive       & SQL, Streaming, MLlib, GraphX \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Hadoop MapReduce against Spark. Spark's advantage is keeping intermediate results in memory, which matters most for the iterative workloads machine learning produces.}
\label{tab:data-hadoop-spark}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Stream and Complex Event Processing}

\begin{v3topic}{Real-Time vs. Near-Real-Time Processing}
    Stream processing handles data in motion — records are processed as they arrive rather than accumulated first\textsuperscript{\cite{leskovecMiningMassiveDatasets2014}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Mode}       & \textbf{Latency}        & \textbf{Examples} \\
        Real-time           & $<$10 ms                & Algorithmic trading, fraud detection \\
        Near-real-time      & Seconds to minutes      & Dashboards, recommendations \\
        Micro-batch         & Seconds (Spark Streaming) & Aggregations with windowing \\
        Batch               & Minutes to hours        & Nightly ETL, model retraining \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Processing modes by latency. Choose from the decision deadline, not from the word ``real-time'' in the requirement.}
\label{tab:data-processing-modes}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Apache Kafka \& Event-Driven Architecture}
    Apache Kafka is a distributed, fault-tolerant, high-throughput message log used as the backbone of event-driven data platforms\textsuperscript{\cite{leskovecMiningMassiveDatasets2014}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Topic}: named channel; producers publish, consumers subscribe
        \item \textbf{Partition}: ordered, immutable log; enables parallelism
        \item \textbf{Consumer group}: each partition consumed by exactly one group member
        \item \textbf{Retention}: replayed from any offset; decouples producers from consumers
        \item \textbf{Exactly-once semantics}: idempotent producers + transactions
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

\subsection{Microservices}

\subsubsection{Introduction to Microservices}

\begin{v3topic}{Monolith vs. Microservices}
    A \emph{monolithic} application deploys all functionality as a single unit; a \emph{microservices} architecture decomposes it into small, independently deployable services each owning its own data\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Aspect}      & \textbf{Monolith}         & \textbf{Microservices} \\
        Deployment unit      & Single artifact           & Many small services \\
        Scaling              & Scale whole app           & Scale per service \\
        Fault isolation      & One bug can crash all     & Failures contained \\
        Development          & Simpler initially         & Requires DevOps maturity \\
        Data storage         & Shared DB                 & DB-per-service \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Monolith against microservices. Microservices trade a deployment problem for a distributed-systems problem; take the trade only when team or scaling boundaries demand it.}
\label{tab:data-monolith-micro}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Benefits \& Trade-offs}
    Microservices excel at scale and team autonomy but introduce distributed systems complexity\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \textbf{Benefits:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Independent deployability and technology choice per service
        \item Fine-grained scalability; resilience through isolation
        \item Small, focused teams (Conway's Law alignment)
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{Trade-offs:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Network latency, distributed transactions, observability overhead
        \item Operational complexity: many services to deploy, monitor, and version
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Implementing Microservices}

\begin{v3topic}{API Gateway \& Service Discovery}
    An \emph{API Gateway} is the single entry point for clients; it routes requests, handles auth, rate-limiting, and SSL termination\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    \emph{Service discovery} lets services find each other dynamically as instances scale up or down.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Client-side discovery}: client queries a registry (e.g., Eureka) and load-balances itself
        \item \textbf{Server-side discovery}: requests go to a load balancer that consults the registry (e.g., AWS ALB + ECS)
        \item Examples: Kong, NGINX, AWS API Gateway; Consul, etcd for registries
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Containerization \& Orchestration}
    \textbf{Docker} packages a service and its dependencies into an immutable, portable \emph{container image}, solving the ``works on my machine'' problem\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    \textbf{Kubernetes} (K8s) orchestrates containers across a cluster: scheduling, self-healing, rolling updates, and service networking.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Docker concept}  & \textbf{Kubernetes equivalent} \\
        Image                     & Pod spec (container definition) \\
        Container                 & Pod (1+ containers) \\
        docker-compose service    & Deployment / StatefulSet \\
        Port mapping              & Service (ClusterIP, NodePort, LoadBalancer) \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Container concepts and their orchestration equivalents.}
\label{tab:data-containers}
\end{v3topic}

\subsection{Governance \& Security}

\subsubsection{Data Protection}

\begin{v3topic}{GDPR Essentials}
    The General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679) governs the processing of personal data of EU residents\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Lawful basis}: consent, contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest
        \item \textbf{Data minimization}: collect only what is necessary
        \item \textbf{Purpose limitation}: use only for the stated purpose
        \item \textbf{Data subject rights}: access, rectification, erasure (right to be forgotten), portability
        \item \textbf{Breach notification}: 72-hour window to inform supervisory authority
        \item \textbf{DPO}: Data Protection Officer required for large-scale processing
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Anonymization \& Pseudonymization}
    Anonymization irreversibly removes the link between data and an individual; pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with tokens while preserving the ability to re-link\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Technique}      & \textbf{Description}                         & \textbf{GDPR status} \\
        Anonymization            & Remove or generalize all identifiers          & Outside GDPR scope \\
        Pseudonymization         & Replace ID with token; key stored separately  & Still personal data \\
        $k$-anonymity            & Each record indistinguishable from $k-1$ others & Privacy model \\
        Differential Privacy     & Add calibrated noise; formal guarantee        & State of the art \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Anonymisation against pseudonymisation, with the GDPR consequence in the last column: only one removes the data from scope, and the bar is higher than it sounds (\cref{ch:it-law}).}
\label{tab:data-anonymisation}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Data Security}

\begin{v3topic}{Encryption at Rest and in Transit}
    \textbf{At rest}: data stored on disk is encrypted using symmetric keys (AES-256 is standard); key management via HSMs or cloud KMS\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    \textbf{In transit}: data moving over networks is protected by TLS 1.2/1.3; mutual TLS (mTLS) authenticates both parties.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Symmetric encryption} (AES): fast, same key for encrypt/decrypt
        \item \textbf{Asymmetric encryption} (RSA, ECDH): key exchange and digital signatures
        \item \textbf{Envelope encryption}: data encrypted with a Data Encryption Key (DEK) which is itself encrypted with a Key Encryption Key (KEK)
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Access Control}
    Access control enforces the \emph{principle of least privilege}: users and services receive only the permissions they need\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Model}   & \textbf{Description} \\
        DAC (Discretionary)    & Owner grants permissions; UNIX file permissions \\
        MAC (Mandatory)        & Labels and clearances; OS-enforced \\
        RBAC (Role-Based)      & Permissions attached to roles, users assigned to roles \\
        ABAC (Attribute-Based) & Policies on user/resource/env attributes; most flexible \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Access-control models, in increasing order of expressiveness and administrative cost.}
\label{tab:data-access-control}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Data Governance}

\begin{v3topic}{Data Quality Dimensions}
    Data governance ensures that data assets are managed, trusted, and used responsibly across an organization\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    Quality is assessed along several dimensions:
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Dimension}   & \textbf{Question} \\
        Accuracy              & Does the data reflect reality? \\
        Completeness          & Are all required values present? \\
        Consistency           & Is the data consistent across systems? \\
        Timeliness            & Is the data up-to-date for its use? \\
        Uniqueness            & Are duplicates controlled? \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Data quality dimensions, each phrased as the question to ask of a dataset before modelling it.}
\label{tab:data-quality}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Data Lineage, Cataloging \& Stewardship}
    \textbf{Data lineage} tracks where data originated and how it was transformed — essential for debugging, audits, and GDPR compliance\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    A \textbf{data catalog} (e.g., Apache Atlas, DataHub) provides a searchable inventory of datasets with metadata, schema, and ownership.
    \textbf{Data stewards} are domain owners accountable for the quality and appropriate use of their datasets.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Lineage tools: Apache Atlas, OpenLineage, dbt lineage graph
        \item Catalog tools: DataHub, Amundsen, AWS Glue Data Catalog
        \item Governance frameworks: DAMA-DMBOK, DCAM
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Defining Principles}

\begin{v3topic}{DataOps: CI/CD for Data}
    DataOps applies DevOps principles — agile development, continuous integration, automated testing — to data pipelines and analytics\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    Goal: shorten the cycle time from data change to validated insight, while improving quality and reliability.
\visualseparator

\subsubsection{Containerization}

\begin{v3topic}{Docker Basics}
    Docker packages application code, runtime, libraries, and configuration into a self-contained \emph{image}\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
    A running instance of an image is a \emph{container} — isolated from the host via Linux namespaces and cgroups.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Dockerfile}: recipe defining the image layer by layer
        \item \textbf{Image registry}: Docker Hub, AWS ECR, GCR — stores and distributes images
        \item \textbf{Immutable infrastructure}: never patch running containers; rebuild and redeploy the image
        \item Benefits for DataOps: reproducible environments, dependency isolation, consistent deployment
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Immutable Infrastructure}
    The \emph{immutable infrastructure} principle states that servers and containers are never modified after deployment — they are replaced\textsuperscript{\cite{gujaStartingDataAnalytics2025}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Mutable (traditional)}  & \textbf{Immutable (container)} \\
        SSH in, patch, restart           & Build new image, deploy, retire old \\
        Configuration drift over time    & Every instance identical to image \\
        Hard to reproduce state          & Full reproducibility from Dockerfile \\
        Rollback: restore backup         & Rollback: redeploy previous image tag \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Mutable against immutable infrastructure. Immutability removes configuration drift, which is the failure that makes ``it works on the other machine'' unanswerable.}
\label{tab:data-immutable}
\end{v3topic}

\subsubsection{Building Data Pipelines}

\begin{v3topic}{Apache Airflow}

\visualseparator

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}

\begin{v3topic}{Measures of Central Tendency}
    Central tendency summarizes the ``typical'' value in a dataset\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Mean}: $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ — sensitive to outliers
        \item \textbf{Median}: middle value when sorted — robust to outliers
        \item \textbf{Mode}: most frequent value — relevant for categorical data
        \item \textbf{Trimmed mean}: mean after removing top/bottom $p\%$; balances mean and median
        \item \textbf{Weighted mean}: $\bar{x}_w = \sum_i w_i x_i / \sum_i w_i$ — used when observations have different importance
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Measures of Spread}
    Spread (dispersion) quantifies how much values deviate from the center\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Measure}       & \textbf{Formula / Notes} \\
        Variance                & $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2$ \\
        Std deviation           & $s = \sqrt{s^2}$; same units as $x$ \\
        IQR                     & $Q_3 - Q_1$; robust to outliers \\
        Range                   & $x_{\max} - x_{\min}$; sensitive to outliers \\
        MAD                     & $\text{median}(|x_i - \text{median}|)$; very robust \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Measures of spread. Standard deviation assumes roughly symmetric data; the interquartile range does not, and survives outliers.}
\label{tab:data-spread}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Five-Number Summary \& Box Plot}
    Tukey's five-number summary provides a robust picture of a univariate distribution\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}:
    $(\min,\; Q_1,\; \text{Median},\; Q_3,\; \max)$.
    The \emph{box plot} visualizes these five numbers: the box spans $Q_1$ to $Q_3$ (IQR), the central line marks the median, and whiskers extend to $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$.
    Points beyond the whiskers are plotted individually as potential outliers.
    Box plots are especially powerful for comparing distributions across multiple groups side-by-side.
\visualseparator
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\sffamily\small]
        % box
        \draw[thick] (2,0) rectangle (4,1);
        % median
        \draw[thick,red] (3,0) -- (3,1);
        % whiskers
        \draw[thick] (1,0.5) -- (2,0.5);
        \draw[thick] (4,0.5) -- (5,0.5);
        % whisker caps
        \draw[thick] (1,0.3) -- (1,0.7);
        \draw[thick] (5,0.3) -- (5,0.7);
        % outlier
        \fill (5.6,0.5) circle (1.5pt);
        % labels
        \node[below] at (1,0) {\small $Q_1{-}1.5\text{IQR}$};
        \node[below] at (2,0) {\small $Q_1$};
        \node[below,red] at (3,0) {\small Med};
        \node[below] at (4,0) {\small $Q_3$};
        \node[below] at (5,0) {\small $Q_3{+}1.5\text{IQR}$};
        \node[below] at (5.6,0) {\small outlier};
    \end{tikzpicture}
\captionof{figure}{The five-number summary and the box plot that encodes it.
The box shows the middle half of the data, so it is robust to the outliers
plotted individually beyond the whiskers --- which is exactly why a box plot
survives skewed data where a mean and standard deviation mislead.}
\label{fig:data-boxplot}
    \end{center}
\end{v3topic}

\subsection{Data Visualization Techniques}

\begin{v3topic}{Chart Selection Guide}
    Choosing the right chart type is the first step in effective communication\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Goal}               & \textbf{Recommended chart} \\
        Distribution of one variable & Histogram, KDE, box plot, violin \\
        Two-variable relationship    & Scatter plot, bubble chart \\
        Composition / proportions    & Pie (few slices), stacked bar \\
        Ranking / comparison         & Bar chart (sort by value) \\
        Trend over time              & Line chart \\
        Correlation matrix           & Heatmap \\
        High-dimensional overview    & Pair plot, parallel coordinates \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Chart choice by analytical goal rather than by data type --- expanded in \cref{ch:visualization}.}
\label{tab:data-chart-selection}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Principles of Effective Visualization}
    Good visualizations maximize the \emph{data-ink ratio}: remove all ink that does not represent data\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Tufte's rule}: erase non-data ink; avoid chartjunk (3-D effects, unnecessary gridlines)
        \item \textbf{Preattentive attributes}: position, color, size are processed instantly; use them for key encodings
        \item \textbf{Aspect ratio}: choose a ratio that highlights the trend; the ``banking to 45°'' guideline for line charts
        \item \textbf{Color}: use perceptually uniform colormaps (viridis, cividis) for continuous data; categorical palettes for discrete groups
        \item \textbf{Annotation}: label the key finding directly on the chart, not only in the caption
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 4.2.3 Correlation Analysis ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Correlation Analysis}

\begin{cautionbox}[What a correlation of zero does not mean]
Pearson correlation measures \emph{linear} association only. Let $X$ be uniform
on $[-1,1]$ and $Y = X^2$. Then $Y$ is completely determined by $X$, yet
\[
  \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]
  = 0 - 0 = 0 ,
\]
so $\rho = 0$. Zero correlation, perfect dependence.

Three more traps in the same family: correlation is destroyed by
non-monotone relationships (use mutual information or a scatter plot);
it is dominated by outliers (a single point can create or erase it); and
different datasets with identical correlation can look nothing alike --- which
is the entire point of Anscombe's quartet. \textbf{Plot the data.} A
correlation coefficient is a summary of a picture you have not looked at.
\end{cautionbox}

\begin{v3topic}{Pearson Correlation}
    Pearson's $r$ measures the strength and direction of the \emph{linear} relationship between two continuous variables\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
    \begin{equation}
        r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i-\bar{y})^2}}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\bar{x},\bar{y}$ are the sample means and $n$ the number of paired
observations; the denominator rescales the covariance so that $r\in[-1,1]$.}
    Range: $r \in [-1, 1]$. $|r| = 1$ indicates perfect linear relationship; $r = 0$ indicates no \emph{linear} relationship (but nonlinear dependence may still exist).

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Spearman Rank Correlation}
    Spearman's $\rho$ is a non-parametric alternative: it computes Pearson's $r$ on the \emph{ranks} of the data\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.
    It is robust to outliers and monotone non-linear relationships.
    \begin{equation}
        \rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum_i d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}
    \end{equation}
    where $d_i$ is the difference in ranks for observation $i$.
    Preferred over Pearson when data is ordinal or heavy-tailed.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Correlation Matrix \& Correlation Is Not Causation}
    A \emph{correlation matrix} displays pairwise correlations for all variable pairs simultaneously; a heatmap visualization quickly reveals clusters of correlated features\textsuperscript{\cite{brucePracticalStatisticsData2017}}.

    \textbf{Correlation does not imply causation.} Three alternative explanations always exist for a correlation between $X$ and $Y$:
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,3cm]|X|}, hlines}
        \textbf{Explanation}         & \textbf{Example} \\
        $X$ causes $Y$                & Ice cream sales $\to$ drownings? (no) \\
        $Y$ causes $X$               & Reverse causation \\
        Confounding variable $Z$      & Hot weather increases both ice cream \emph{and} swimming \\
        Spurious correlation          & Nicolas Cage films $\sim$ pool drownings (coincidence) \\
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Why correlation does not establish causation, with the alternative explanation for each case. A fourth possibility is always present: coincidence.}
\label{tab:data-correlation-causation}
    Establishing causation requires randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or causal inference methods (Pearl's do-calculus, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity).
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 4.2.4 Dimensionality Reduction -----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction}

\begin{workedexample}[PCA on a $2\times2$ covariance matrix, by hand]
Two standardised features with covariance matrix
\[
  \Sigma = \begin{pNiceMatrix} 1 & 0.8\\ 0.8 & 1 \end{pNiceMatrix}.
\]

\emph{Eigenvalues.} $\det(\Sigma-\lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 0.64 = 0$, so
$1-\lambda = \pm0.8$ and
\[
  \lambda_1 = 1.8,\qquad \lambda_2 = 0.2 .
\]
Check against the trace: $1.8+0.2 = 2 = 1+1$. \checkmark

\emph{Eigenvectors.} For $\lambda_1 = 1.8$: $(1-1.8)v_1 + 0.8v_2 = 0
\Rightarrow v_2 = v_1$, giving the direction $(1,1)/\sqrt2$. For
$\lambda_2 = 0.2$: $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$.

\emph{Explained variance.}
\[
  \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2} = \frac{1.8}{2.0} = 90\,\% ,
  \qquad \text{second component } 10\,\% .
\]

\emph{Read it.} Because the two features are strongly correlated, almost all
the variation lies along their sum. Keeping one component discards $10\,\%$ of
the variance and halves the dimension. Note what the components \emph{are}: the
first is ``both features together'', the second is ``the difference between
them''. PCA components are combinations of your original variables, so they
usually have no domain meaning --- which is the price of the compression, and
the reason PCA hurts interpretability even when it helps performance.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{cautionbox}[PCA maximises variance, not usefulness]
PCA has no access to your labels. A direction with small variance can carry all
the discriminative signal, and PCA will discard it first. It is also scale
dependent --- run it on unstandardised data and whichever feature happens to be
measured in the largest units becomes the first component regardless of
importance. Standardise first, and if the goal is class separation rather than
compression, use a supervised method instead.
\end{cautionbox}

\begin{v3topic}{PCA Step-by-Step}
    Principal Component Analysis finds a lower-dimensional linear subspace that captures maximum variance\textsuperscript{\cite{hastieElementsStatisticalLearning2008}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Center} the data: subtract the column means so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}] = \mathbf{0}$
        \item (Optionally \textbf{scale}: divide by column std to get unit variance)
        \item \textbf{Compute} the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$
        \item \textbf{Eigendecompose}: $\mathbf{C} =
              \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^\top$; columns of
              $\mathbf{V}$ are principal directions
        \item \textbf{Sort} eigenvectors by descending eigenvalue $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots$
        \item \textbf{Project}: $\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{V}_k$ retains the top-$k$ components
        \item \textbf{Explained variance ratio}: $\frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_i \lambda_i}$; choose $k$ so cumulative ratio $\geq$ 95\%
    \end{enumerate}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{t-SNE \& UMAP}
    For high-dimensional data, linear PCA often fails to reveal cluster structure visible to non-linear methods\textsuperscript{\cite{hastieElementsStatisticalLearning2008}}.
\visualseparator
    \textbf{t-SNE} (van der Maaten \& Hinton, 2008):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Models pairwise similarities as Gaussians in high-D, Student-$t$ in low-D
        \item Minimizes KL divergence between the two distributions via gradient descent
        \item Preserves local structure extremely well; global distances not preserved
        \item Stochastic; different runs give different layouts; use for visualization only
    \end{itemize}
    \textbf{UMAP} (McInnes et al., 2018):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Fuzzy topological data analysis; builds and aligns $k$-nearest-neighbor graphs
        \item Faster than t-SNE; better global structure preservation
        \item Supports new-point embedding (transform); useful for pipelines
    \end{itemize}
\end{v3topic}

\section{Deep Dive: Data Contracts, Lineage, and Drift}
\begin{v3topic}{A Schema Is Not a Data Contract}
A contract includes semantics, units, allowed values, null meaning, freshness,
uniqueness, privacy classification, owners, change policy, and service level.
Two columns can share a type while representing incompatible populations or
measurement procedures.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Lineage Makes Errors Reversible}
Record source, transformation version, validation result, and downstream
consumers for every material dataset and feature.
Lineage should answer which models used a corrupted source and which reports
must be recomputed after a correction.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Drift Is a Question, Not One Score}
Distinguish input drift $p(x)$, label drift $p(y)$, concept drift $p(y\mid x)$,
and operational changes in collection or policy.
A PSI or KS alert can indicate distribution change but cannot identify cause or
business impact by itself.
Pair statistical signals with slice-level performance, data-quality checks,
and an investigation playbook.
\end{v3topic}


\begin{practicallab}[Audit a dataset before you model it]
\textbf{Goal.} Produce the one-page data report that should precede every
modelling decision --- and that most projects skip.

\textbf{Protocol.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Shape and types.} Row count, column count, dtype per column,
        memory. Flag any numeric column stored as text.
  \item \textbf{Missingness.} Percentage missing per column, and the
        co-occurrence pattern between columns. Classify each as MCAR, MAR, or
        MNAR and justify the choice in one sentence.
  \item \textbf{Duplicates.} Exact duplicate rows, and near-duplicates on the
        key columns. Decide whether they are genuine repeats or a join gone
        wrong.
  \item \textbf{Distributions.} For every numeric column: min, max, median,
        IQR, and a histogram. Look for impossible values --- negative ages,
        future timestamps, placeholder codes such as $-999$ or $0$ standing in
        for missing.
  \item \textbf{Cardinality.} For every categorical column: distinct count and
        the top ten values. Anything with cardinality near the row count is an
        identifier and must not be a feature.
  \item \textbf{Target leakage sweep.} Correlate every feature with the target.
        Investigate anything suspiciously strong --- it is usually a column
        computed \emph{after} the outcome was known.
  \item \textbf{Time and groups.} Is there a timestamp? Do rows share an entity?
        Either one changes how you must split.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Deliverable.} A one-page report ending in two sentences: the split
strategy this data requires, and the single biggest risk you found.
\textbf{The point:} step 6 has saved more projects than any modelling choice in
this book.
\end{practicallab}

\begin{checkpoint}
A model's accuracy falls from $0.91$ to $0.78$ over six months in production
while the code is unchanged. Name three distinct mechanisms that could cause
this --- one about the inputs, one about the relationship between inputs and
target, and one about the labels --- and give the monitor that detects each.
\end{checkpoint}
