\begin{learningobjectives}
After working through this chapter you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item state, for any method here, its inputs, its assumptions, and the
        failure it cannot survive;
  \item compute information gain for a candidate tree split by hand;
  \item trace $k$-means to convergence on a small dataset and explain why the
        initialisation matters;
  \item read a confusion matrix and say why $94\,\%$ accuracy can be worse than
        useless;
  \item explain why ridge shrinks coefficients but lasso sets them to exactly
        zero, with the arithmetic that shows it;
  \item choose $k$ in $k$-NN from a bias--variance argument rather than a
        default;
  \item distinguish bagging from boosting by what each one reduces;
  \item design an honest evaluation, including the split, the baseline, and the
        leak you are most likely to introduce.
\end{itemize}
\end{learningobjectives}

\begin{plainlanguage}
Classical machine learning is a toolbox of ways to draw a line, a curve, or a
boundary through data. Some methods draw one straight line and try to place it
well (regression, SVM). Some carve the space into boxes with yes/no questions
(trees). Some just remember every example and ask the neighbours ($k$-NN). Some
look for clumps without being told what to look for (clustering). Choosing
among them is mostly a question of what shape you believe the answer has, and
how much data you have to justify that belief.
\end{plainlanguage}

\begin{engineernote}
Read this chapter with one question always in front of you: \emph{what would
have to be true for this method to work?} Every algorithm here encodes an
assumption --- that classes are linearly separable, that clusters are
spherical, that nearby points share labels, that features are independent.
Methods do not fail because they are weak. They fail because their assumption
was false for your data and nobody checked.
\end{engineernote}

\section{Introduction to Machine Learning}

% ---- 5.1.1 What is Machine Learning? -----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What is Machine Learning?}

\begin{v3topic}{What is Machine Learning?}
    Machine learning (ML) is the study of algorithms that improve their performance at a task through experience\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~8--10]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
    Rather than being explicitly programmed with rules, an ML algorithm learns patterns from data.
    A learning algorithm receives training examples and produces a model (also called a hypothesis) that can make predictions on previously unseen inputs.

\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.1.2 Supervised & Unsupervised Learning --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Supervised \& Unsupervised Learning}

\begin{v3topic}{Supervised Learning}
    In supervised learning, the training data consists of input--output pairs $(\mathbf{x}_n, y_n)$\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~8--12]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
    The algorithm learns a mapping $f: \mathbf{x} \mapsto y$ that generalizes to unseen inputs.
    The two main supervised tasks are \textbf{classification} (discrete $y$) and \textbf{regression} (continuous $y$).
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Labels provided during training
        \item Optimizes a loss w.r.t.\ known outputs
        \item Examples: logistic regression, SVM, random forest, neural networks
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Unsupervised Learning}
    In unsupervised learning, only the inputs $\mathbf{x}_n$ are given---there are no labels\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.~166]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
    The algorithm must discover hidden structure such as clusters, latent factors, or density estimates.
\visualseparator
    \begin{itemize}
        \item No labels; discovers structure from data alone
        \item Optimizes cohesion/separation or likelihood
        \item Examples: K-means, DBSCAN, PCA, autoencoders
    \end{itemize}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Classification vs.\ Clustering}
    Classification and clustering\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~323--324]{hurbansGrokkingArtificialIntelligence2020}} both produce groups, but they differ fundamentally in their inputs and their objectives.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[c,m]|X|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Aspect}     & \textbf{Classification}                       & \textbf{Clustering}                                  \\
        \hline
        Learning type       & Supervised (with labels)                      & Unsupervised (without labels)                        \\
        \hline
        Main question       & ``Which known class does $x$ belong to?''     & ``What natural groups exist in the data?''           \\
        \hline
        Output              & Predefined class labels                       & Newly discovered clusters                            \\
        \hline
        Typical algorithms  & Logistic regression, SVM, random forest, k-NN & K-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical clustering, GMM        \\
        \hline
        Evaluation          & Accuracy, F1, ROC-AUC                         & Silhouette, within-cluster variance, domain judgment \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Classification and clustering both produce groups, but only
one of them is told in advance what the groups mean. That difference decides
how you are allowed to evaluate the result.}
\label{tab:classification-vs-clustering}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.1.3 Regression & Classification ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Regression \& Classification}

\begin{v3topic}{Regression}
    Regression predicts a continuous real-valued output.
    The model minimizes expected loss over the data distribution\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~8--9]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
    Typical loss functions include squared error and absolute error.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{\varepsilon} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(y_n - f(\mathbf{x}_n))^2
    \end{equation}
\where{$N$ is the number of examples, $y_n$ the true target for example $n$,
and $f(\mathbf{x}_n)$ the model's prediction for its input $\mathbf{x}_n$.}
    Evaluation: MSE, RMSE, MAE, $R^2$.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Classification}
    Classification predicts a discrete class label.
    The model learns a decision boundary separating the classes\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~10--12]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
    The ideal loss is zero/one, but smooth surrogates (logistic loss, hinge loss) are used in practice.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \ell^{(0/1)}(y, \hat{y}) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } y = \hat{y} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\where{$y$ is the true class and $\hat{y}$ the predicted one; the loss is $1$
for any mistake regardless of how confident or how close it was, which is what
makes it non-differentiable and forces the use of surrogates.}
    Evaluation: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, ROC-AUC.
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.1.5 Model Evaluation & Overfitting ------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model Evaluation \& Overfitting}

\begin{v3topic}{Bias--Variance Trade-off}
    A model's prediction error decomposes into three components: bias (systematic error from simplifying assumptions), variance (sensitivity to training data fluctuations), and irreducible noise\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~14--25]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
    \emph{Underfitting} (high bias) occurs when the model is too simple; \emph{overfitting} (high variance) occurs when the model memorizes noise in the training data.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Error} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Irreducible Noise}
        \label{eq:ml-bias-variance}
    \end{equation}
\where{\emph{bias} is the error from the model being too simple to represent
the truth, \emph{variance} the error from sensitivity to the particular training
sample, and \emph{irreducible noise} the part no model can remove.}

\begin{plainlanguage}
Bias is being consistently wrong in the same direction --- your model is too
simple to see the pattern, like fitting a straight line to a curve. Variance is
being wrong in a different direction every time --- your model is so flexible
it chases the noise, and a slightly different training set would give a
completely different answer. Irreducible noise is the part nobody can fix,
because the data does not contain the answer.
\end{plainlanguage}

--- the double-descent behaviour described in
\cref{ch:deep-learning}. Use the U-curve to reason about a decision tree's
depth or a polynomial's degree, where it holds well. Do not use it to argue
that a larger neural network must generalise worse.
\end{cautionbox}

\begin{v3topic}{Train/Validation/Test Split}
    To estimate generalization performance, data is split into three disjoint sets\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~18--20]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Training set:} used to fit model parameters
        \item \textbf{Validation set:} used to tune hyperparameters and select models
        \item \textbf{Test set:} held out until the very end to estimate true performance
    \end{itemize}
    A common split ratio is 60/20/20 or 70/15/15.

\begin{cautionbox}[The test set is a budget, and you can only spend it once]
Every time you look at the test score and then change something, you leak
information from the test set into your model, and the score stops being an
unbiased estimate. After twenty such rounds you have effectively fitted the
test set by hand.

Three further leaks are more subtle and more common:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Preprocessing before splitting.} Computing a mean, a scaler, or
        a vocabulary on the full dataset and \emph{then} splitting leaks the
        test distribution into training. Fit every transform on the training
        fold only.
  \item \textbf{Random splits on grouped data.} If one patient, user, or
        machine contributes several rows, a random split puts near-duplicates
        on both sides. Split by group.
  \item \textbf{Random splits on time series.} A random split lets the model
        learn from the future. Split chronologically.
\end{itemize}
Each of these produces a validation score that looks excellent and a deployment
that disappoints, which is the single most common way a machine-learning
project fails.
\end{cautionbox}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Cross-Validation}
    $k$-fold cross-validation partitions the data into $k$ equal folds.
    The model is trained $k$ times, each time using a different fold as validation and the remaining $k{-}1$ folds for training\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~20--22]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \text{CV}(k) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{Error}_i
        \label{eq:ml-cv}
    \end{equation}
\where{$k$ is the number of folds and $\text{Error}_i$ the error measured on
fold $i$ by a model trained on the other $k-1$ folds.}
    Typical choice: $k = 5$ or $k = 10$.

\begin{workedexample}[Reading a cross-validation result honestly]
Five folds return accuracies $0.86,\ 0.91,\ 0.78,\ 0.89,\ 0.86$.

\emph{Mean.} $\dfrac{0.86+0.91+0.78+0.89+0.86}{5} = \dfrac{4.30}{5} = 0.860$.

\emph{Standard deviation.} Deviations are
$0.00,\ +0.05,\ -0.08,\ +0.03,\ 0.00$; their squares are
$0,\ 0.0025,\ 0.0064,\ 0.0009,\ 0$, summing to $0.0098$. Dividing by
$k-1 = 4$ gives $0.00245$, so $s = 0.0495$.

\emph{Report.} ``$0.86 \pm 0.05$ over 5 folds'', not ``$0.86$''.

\emph{Now interpret.} A competing model scores $0.88$. Is it better? The
difference is $0.02$, well inside a spread of $0.05$. On this evidence you
cannot tell the two apart. The fold at $0.78$ is also worth a look on its own:
one fold performing far below the others usually means that fold contains a
subgroup the model handles badly, and that is more informative than the mean.
\end{workedexample}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.1.6 Measuring a Classifier -------------------------------------------
\subsection{Measuring a Classifier}

\begin{v3topic}{The Confusion Matrix}
    Every binary classification result is one of four outcomes. Arranging them
    in a $2\times2$ table gives the \keyterm{confusion matrix}, from which every
    other classification metric is derived.
\visualseparator
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[
      cell/.style={draw=AlmanachInk!35,minimum width=27mm,minimum height=12mm,
        font=\sffamily\small,align=center},
      good/.style={cell,fill=AlmanachTeal!16},
      bad/.style={cell,fill=AlmanachRed!16},
      lbl/.style={font=\sffamily\scriptsize,color=AlmanachInk!75}]
      \node[good] (tp) at (0,0) {\textbf{TP}\\80};
      \node[bad]  (fp) at (27mm,0) {\textbf{FP}\\20};
      \node[bad]  (fn) at (0,-12mm) {\textbf{FN}\\40};
      \node[good] (tn) at (27mm,-12mm) {\textbf{TN}\\860};
      \node[lbl,above] at (0,7mm) {predicted positive};
      \node[lbl,above] at (27mm,7mm) {predicted negative};
      \node[lbl,rotate=90,anchor=south] at (-16mm,0) {actually positive};
      \node[lbl,rotate=90,anchor=south] at (-16mm,-12mm) {actually negative};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \captionof{figure}{A confusion matrix for 1000 cases with 120 true
    positives. The two off-diagonal cells are different mistakes with different
    costs, and no single number can represent both.}
    \label{fig:ml-confusion-matrix}
    \end{center}
\end{v3topic}

\begin{v3topic}{Precision, Recall, and the Accuracy Trap}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},
        \qquad
        \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},
        \qquad
        F_1 = \frac{2\,TP}{2\,TP+FP+FN}.
        \label{eq:ml-precision-recall}
    \end{equation}
\where{$TP$ counts correctly flagged positives, $FP$ wrongly flagged
negatives, and $FN$ missed positives. Precision divides by everything the model
flagged; recall divides by everything it should have flagged.}

\begin{plainlanguage}
Precision answers ``when the model raised its hand, how often was it right?''
Recall answers ``of everything it should have caught, how much did it catch?''
You can always raise one by sacrificing the other: predict positive for
everything and recall hits $100\,\%$ while precision collapses. The $F_1$ score
is their harmonic mean, which punishes a large gap between the two.
\end{plainlanguage}

\begin{workedexample}[Why $94\,\%$ accuracy can be a failing grade]
Take the numbers in \cref{fig:ml-confusion-matrix}: $TP=80$, $FP=20$, $FN=40$,
$TN=860$, out of $1000$ cases of which $120$ are truly positive.
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \text{Accuracy} &= \frac{80+860}{1000} = 0.940, &
  \text{Precision} &= \frac{80}{80+20} = 0.800,\\[3pt]
  \text{Recall} &= \frac{80}{80+40} = 0.667, &
  F_1 &= \frac{160}{160+20+40} = \frac{160}{220} = 0.727 .
\end{aligned}
\]

\emph{Now compute the baseline.} A model that predicts ``negative'' for every
single case, and therefore does nothing at all, scores
\[
  \text{Accuracy}_{\text{trivial}} = \frac{880}{1000} = 0.880 .
\]

So the real model's $94\,\%$ buys $6$ percentage points over a model with no
information in it, while missing a third of the positive cases. Reported as
``$94\,\%$ accurate'', this sounds like a success. Reported as ``catches two
out of three, one in five alarms is false, against an $88\,\%$ do-nothing
baseline'', it sounds like what it is.

\emph{The rule.} Never report accuracy without the majority-class baseline
beside it. On imbalanced data, accuracy measures the imbalance more than it
measures the model.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{engineernote}
Which metric to optimise is a question about consequences, not statistics. In
cancer screening a false negative may cost a life while a false positive costs
an anxious week and a second test, so recall dominates. In an email spam filter
a false positive may delete a job offer, so precision dominates. Decide this
\emph{before} you train, write it down, and let it choose the threshold.
\Cref{ch:evaluation-mlops} develops the full protocol.
\end{engineernote}
\end{v3topic}

\begin{checkpoint}
A fraud model flags $500$ of $100{,}000$ transactions; $50$ flags are genuine
fraud, and $30$ frauds were missed. Compute precision, recall, and $F_1$. Then
state which of the two errors you would rather halve, and what that choice does
to the other.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{Clustering}

% ---- 5.2.1 Introduction to Clustering ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Introduction to Clustering}

\begin{v3topic}{What is Clustering?}
    Clustering is unsupervised learning that partitions data into groups (clusters) such that objects within a cluster are more similar to each other than to objects in other clusters\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.166]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Unlike classification, there are no predefined class labels; the algorithm discovers structure from the data alone.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Clustering Families}
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[c,m]|X|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Family}      & \textbf{Representative Algorithm} & \textbf{Key Idea}                          \\
        \hline
        Partitioning         & K-Means                           & Minimize within-cluster sum of squares     \\
        \hline
        Hierarchical         & Agglomerative / Divisive          & Build nested cluster tree (dendrogram)     \\
        \hline
        Density-based        & DBSCAN                            & Clusters as dense, connected regions       \\
        \hline
        Model-based          & GMM / EM                          & Mixture of Gaussian distributions          \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Four families of clustering algorithm. They differ mainly in
what they assume a cluster \emph{is} --- a centroid, a nesting, a dense region,
or a probability distribution --- and that assumption decides which datasets
each one can handle.}
\label{tab:ml-clustering-families}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Evaluating Cluster Quality}
    Because there are no labels, clustering quality must be assessed by internal criteria\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~443--445]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. The silhouette coefficient $s(i)$ combines cohesion (average intra-cluster distance $a(i)$) and separation (average distance to the nearest other cluster $b(i)$) into a single score in $[-1, 1]$.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i),\, b(i)\}}, \qquad s(i) \in [-1,\, 1]
        \label{eq:ml-silhouette}
    \end{equation}
\where{$a(i)$ is point $i$'s mean distance to its own cluster and $b(i)$ its
mean distance to the nearest other cluster; the denominator normalises so the
score cannot exceed one in magnitude.}
    The measure is due to
    Rousseeuw\textsuperscript{\cite{rousseeuwSilhouettesGraphicalAid1987}}.

\begin{workedexample}[Reading a silhouette score]
A point has mean distance $a(i) = 1.2$ to its own cluster and $b(i) = 3.6$ to
the nearest other cluster:
\[
  s(i) = \frac{3.6-1.2}{\max\{1.2,\,3.6\}} = \frac{2.4}{3.6} = +0.67 .
\]
Comfortably assigned. Now a second point with $a(i) = 3.0$ and $b(i) = 2.4$:
\[
  s(i) = \frac{2.4-3.0}{3.0} = -0.20 .
\]
Negative, which means this point is on average \emph{closer} to a neighbouring
cluster than to its own --- it is probably misassigned. That is the practical
value of the silhouette: it is computed per point, so it localises the problem
instead of only scoring the partition.
\end{workedexample}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.2.2 K-Means -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{K-Means}

\begin{v3topic}{K-Means Objective}
    K-means partitions $N$ data points into $K$ clusters by minimizing the within-cluster sum of squared distances to the cluster centroid\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.166]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The algorithm alternates between assigning each point to its nearest centroid and recomputing each centroid as the mean of its assigned points.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{z}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{n:\, z_n = k} \|\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2
    \end{equation}
\where{$K$ is the number of clusters, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ the centroid of
cluster $k$, $\mathbf{x}_n$ the $n$-th data point, and $z_n$ the index of the
cluster it is assigned to.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{K-Means Algorithm}
    K-means is guaranteed to converge (the objective decreases monotonically at each step) but only to a local optimum\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~166--168]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Initialization therefore strongly affects the solution quality.
\visualseparator
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Initialize $K$ cluster centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_K$
        \item \textbf{Assign:} $z_n \leftarrow \arg\min_k \|\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2$ for every point $n$
        \item \textbf{Update:} $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k \leftarrow \dfrac{1}{|C_k|}\displaystyle\sum_{n \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_n$ for every cluster $k$
        \item Repeat steps 2--3 until convergence (no assignment changes)
    \end{enumerate}
    The procedure is Lloyd's
    algorithm\textsuperscript{\cite{lloydLeastSquaresQuantization1982}}.

\begin{workedexample}[$k$-means traced to convergence]
Cluster the one-dimensional points $\{1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\,9\}$ with $K=2$, starting
from $\mu_1 = 1$ and $\mu_2 = 9$.

\emph{Iteration 1 --- assign.} Compare each point's distance to both centroids:

\smallskip
\begin{tblr}{
  width=0.72\linewidth,
  colspec={Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]},
  row{1}={font=\bfseries,bg=AlmanachMist},
  hlines,vlines}
point & $|x-\mu_1|$ & $|x-\mu_2|$ & assigned \\
1 & 0 & 8 & $C_1$ \\
2 & 1 & 7 & $C_1$ \\
4 & 3 & 5 & $C_1$ \\
8 & 7 & 1 & $C_2$ \\
9 & 8 & 0 & $C_2$ \\
\end{tblr}
\smallskip

\emph{Iteration 1 --- update.}
$\mu_1 = \frac{1+2+4}{3} = 2.333$ and $\mu_2 = \frac{8+9}{2} = 8.5$.

\emph{Iteration 2 --- assign.} Point $4$ is the only one worth rechecking:
$|4-2.333| = 1.667$ against $|4-8.5| = 4.5$, so it stays in $C_1$. No
assignment changes, so the algorithm has converged.

\emph{Final objective.}
\[
  \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{(1{-}2.333)^2+(2{-}2.333)^2+(4{-}2.333)^2}_{4.667}
  + \underbrace{(8{-}8.5)^2+(9{-}8.5)^2}_{0.5} = 5.167 .
\]

\emph{The catch.} This converged in two iterations to an obviously sensible
answer --- because the initial centroids happened to sit at the two extremes.
$k$-means only guarantees convergence to a \emph{local} optimum of
$\mathcal{L}$, and a poor initialisation can converge just as confidently to a
worse partition. That is the entire motivation for $k$-means++.
\end{workedexample}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{K-Means++ Initialization}
    K-means++ improves the standard random initialization by spreading the initial centroids across the data\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~168--169]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The first centroid is chosen uniformly at random; each subsequent centroid is chosen with probability proportional to $D(\mathbf{x})^2$, the squared distance to the nearest existing centroid. This yields the approximation guarantee below.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{\mathcal{L}}\bigr] \leq 8(\log K + 2)\,\mathcal{L}^{(\mathrm{opt})}
        \label{eq:ml-kmeanspp}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\hat{\mathcal{L}}$ is the objective reached from a $k$-means++ seeding
and $\mathcal{L}^{(\mathrm{opt})}$ the best achievable value; the expectation is
over the randomness in the seeding.}
    The seeding scheme and this guarantee are due to Arthur and
    Vassilvitskii\textsuperscript{\cite{arthurKmeansAdvantagesCareful2007}}.

\begin{cautionbox}[$k$-means assumes round clusters of similar size]
The objective minimises squared Euclidean distance to a centroid, which means
it can only find clusters that are roughly spherical and roughly equal in
extent. Give it two elongated parallel bands and it will cut across them; give
it one dense cluster and one diffuse one and it will steal points from the
diffuse one. It will also always return exactly $K$ clusters, including when
the data has none. If your clusters might be elongated, use a Gaussian mixture;
if they might be irregular or absent, use DBSCAN.
\end{cautionbox}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Choosing K: Elbow Method and Information Criteria}
    There is no single best method for choosing $K$\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.169]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The elbow method plots the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) against $K$ and selects the value at which improvement begins to plateau. For probabilistic models such as Gaussian mixtures, information criteria penalise model complexity explicitly; they require a fitted likelihood rather than raw WCSS.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \operatorname{AIC} = -2\log \hat L + 2p,
        \qquad
        \operatorname{BIC} = -2\log \hat L + p\log N
    \end{equation}
    Here $\hat L$ is the maximised likelihood, $p$ is the number of fitted parameters, and $N$ is the number of independent observations. Lower values are preferred only among models fitted to the same response data under comparable likelihood definitions.
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.2.3 Expectation Maximization ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Expectation Maximization}

\begin{v3topic}{Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)}
    A Gaussian Mixture Model assumes data are generated from $K$ Gaussian components with mixing weights $\theta_k$, means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$, and covariances $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.175]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Unlike K-means (hard, binary assignments), GMM fitting via EM produces \emph{soft} (probabilistic) memberships: each point belongs to every cluster to some degree.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_k \,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k), \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_k = 1
    \end{equation}
\where{$\theta_k$ is the mixing weight of component $k$ (how much of the data
it accounts for), $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ its mean vector and
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$ its covariance matrix.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{E-Step: Computing Responsibilities}
    In the Expectation step, the current parameters are held fixed and the posterior probability (responsibility) that component $k$ generated point $\mathbf{x}_n$ is computed\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~175--177]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. These soft assignments $z_{n,k}$ replace the hard assignments $z_n$ of K-means.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        z_{n,k} = \frac{\theta_k \,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)}
                       {\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K} \theta_j \,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)}
        \label{eq:ml-em-estep}
    \end{equation}
\where{$z_{n,k}\in[0,1]$ is the \emph{responsibility} of component $k$ for
point $n$; the denominator sums the same quantity over all $K$ components, so
the responsibilities for one point sum to one.}
    The algorithm and its convergence proof are due to Dempster, Laird and
    Rubin\textsuperscript{\cite{dempsterMaximumLikelihoodIncomplete1977}}.

\begin{workedexample}[One responsibility, computed]
Two one-dimensional components with equal weights $\theta_1=\theta_2=0.5$,
means $\mu_1 = 0$ and $\mu_2 = 4$, and unit variances. Where does the point
$x = 1$ belong?

\emph{Component densities.} With
$\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu,1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2}$ and
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} = 0.39894$:
\[
  \mathcal{N}(1\mid 0,1) = 0.39894\,e^{-0.5} = 0.39894(0.60653) = 0.24197,
\]
\[
  \mathcal{N}(1\mid 4,1) = 0.39894\,e^{-4.5} = 0.39894(0.011109) = 0.004432 .
\]

\emph{Responsibilities.}
\[
  z_{1} = \frac{0.5(0.24197)}{0.5(0.24197)+0.5(0.004432)}
        = \frac{0.24197}{0.24640} = 0.982,
  \qquad z_2 = 0.018 .
\]
Check: $0.982+0.018 = 1.000$. \checkmark

\emph{Read it.} The point belongs $98.2\,\%$ to the first component and
$1.8\,\%$ to the second. $k$-means would have said ``cluster 1'' and thrown the
$1.8\,\%$ away. That retained fraction is what lets EM express uncertainty
about points lying between clusters --- and it is why EM can report that a
point is genuinely ambiguous where $k$-means cannot.
\end{workedexample}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{M-Step: Re-estimating Parameters}
    In the Maximization step, the responsibilities are held fixed and the model parameters are updated to maximize the expected log-likelihood\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~177--178]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Each iteration of EM is guaranteed to increase (or maintain) the log-likelihood.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \theta_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} z_{n,k}, \qquad
        \boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{\sum_n z_{n,k}\,\mathbf{x}_n}{\sum_n z_{n,k}}, \qquad
        \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k = \frac{\sum_n z_{n,k}(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top}{\sum_n z_{n,k}}
    \end{equation}
\where{each update is the ordinary weighted estimate --- weight, mean, and
covariance --- with the responsibilities $z_{n,k}$ from the E-step used as the
weights instead of hard cluster memberships.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{EM as a Generalization of K-Means}
    K-means is a special case of EM\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.180]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. When all GMM covariances are fixed to $\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$ and $\sigma \to 0$, the soft responsibilities $z_{n,k}$ collapse to hard indicator assignments and the EM update reduces exactly to the K-means update. EM thus trades the assumption of spherical, equal-size clusters for the ability to model elliptical clusters of varying shapes and sizes.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|X|X|X|}}
        \hline
                                & \textbf{K-Means}          & \textbf{EM / GMM}                    \\
        \hline
        Assignments             & Hard (binary)             & Soft (probabilistic)                 \\
        \hline
        Cluster shape           & Spherical, equal-size     & Elliptical, varying size             \\
        \hline
        Parameters learned      & Centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ & $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$, $\theta_k$ \\
        \hline
        Convergence guarantee   & Local optimum of WCSS     & Monotone increase of log-likelihood  \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{$k$-means is the zero-variance limit of a Gaussian mixture.
Every row is a capability EM buys with extra parameters --- and extra
parameters are exactly what makes it slower and more prone to poor local
optima.}
\label{tab:ml-kmeans-vs-em}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.2.4 DBSCAN ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{DBSCAN}

\begin{v3topic}{Density-Based Clustering Concept}
    DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) identifies clusters as dense regions of points separated by sparser regions\textsuperscript{\cite{esterDensityBasedAlgorithmDiscovering1996}, \cite[][p.~471]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. It requires two parameters: the neighborhood radius $\varepsilon$ and the minimum point count MinPts. Crucially, DBSCAN requires no preset number of clusters $K$ and naturally labels low-density points as noise (outliers).

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Core, Border, and Noise Points}
    Every point in DBSCAN is classified as exactly one of three types based on the density of its $\varepsilon$-neighborhood $N_\varepsilon(p) = \{q \mid d(p,q) \leq \varepsilon\}$\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~472--474]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. A cluster is the maximal set of density-connected core and border points.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[c,m]|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Point type} & \textbf{Definition}                                                          \\
        \hline
        Core point          & $|N_\varepsilon(p)| \geq \text{MinPts}$ — enough neighbors to start a cluster \\
        \hline
        Border point        & $|N_\varepsilon(p)| < \text{MinPts}$ but within $\varepsilon$ of a core point  \\
        \hline
        Noise point         & Neither core nor border — an outlier                                         \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{The three point types in DBSCAN. Only core points can start
or extend a cluster, which is what lets the algorithm refuse to assign an
outlier at all --- something $k$-means cannot do.}
\label{tab:ml-dbscan-point-types}

\end{v3topic}

    With MinPts $= 4$ and radius $\varepsilon$, core points have dense neighborhoods, border points are nearby but sparse, and noise points are isolated\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.473]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.

\begin{engineernote}
DBSCAN replaces the question ``how many clusters?'' with the question ``how
dense is dense?'', which is often easier to answer from domain knowledge but is
not free. A common heuristic sets MinPts $\approx 2D$ for $D$ dimensions, then
chooses $\varepsilon$ from the knee of a sorted $k$-distance plot. Two
limitations matter in practice: a single global $\varepsilon$ cannot handle
clusters of genuinely different densities, and in high dimensions the distance
concentration described in \cref{ch:mathematical-foundations} makes
$\varepsilon$ progressively harder to set at all.
\end{engineernote}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{DBSCAN Algorithm}
    DBSCAN visits each unvisited point, expands a new cluster if a core point is found, and marks isolated points as noise\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~474--477]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. With a spatial index (e.g.\ R-tree), complexity is $O(n \log n)$; without indexing it degrades to $O(n^2)$.
\visualseparator
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For each unvisited point $p$: compute $N_\varepsilon(p)$
        \item If $|N_\varepsilon(p)| \geq \text{MinPts}$: mark $p$ as a core point, start a new cluster $C$
        \item Expand $C$: add all points density-reachable from $p$ (recursively include core points found)
        \item If $|N_\varepsilon(p)| < \text{MinPts}$: mark $p$ as noise (may later become a border point of another cluster)
        \item Repeat until all points are visited
    \end{enumerate}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.2.5 Hierarchical Clustering -------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hierarchical Clustering}

\begin{v3topic}{Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering}
    Hierarchical clustering builds a tree-like decomposition of data called a dendrogram\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.457]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. The agglomerative (bottom-up) approach starts with each data point as its own singleton cluster and iteratively merges the two closest clusters until only one remains. The number of clusters $K$ need not be specified in advance: it is determined by cutting the dendrogram at the desired height.
\visualseparator
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Start: assign each of the $n$ points to its own cluster
        \item Compute the $n \times n$ pairwise distance matrix
        \item Merge the two clusters with the smallest inter-cluster distance
        \item Update the distance matrix (recompute distances to the merged cluster)
        \item Repeat steps 3--4 until one cluster remains
        \item Cut the dendrogram at the desired level to obtain $K$ clusters
    \end{enumerate}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Linkage Criteria}
    The choice of linkage criterion defines how the distance between two clusters $C_i$ and $C_j$ is measured and strongly affects the shape of the resulting clusters\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~460--463]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.
\visualseparator
    \begin{description}
        \item[Single:] minimum pairwise distance; it can form elongated
        ``chains.''
        \item[Complete:] maximum pairwise distance; it favours compact
        clusters.
        \item[Average (UPGMA):]
        $\frac{1}{|C_i||C_j|}\sum_{p\in C_i,\,q\in C_j}d(p,q)$; a compromise
        between single and complete linkage.
        \item[Ward:] merge the pair causing the smallest increase in total
        within-cluster variance; it often favours compact, similarly sized
        clusters.
    \end{description}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Reading a Dendrogram}
    The height of each merge in the dendrogram encodes the inter-cluster distance at which two groups were joined\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~457--467]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. A horizontal cut at height $h$ produces the clustering at that granularity: every connected subtree below the cut is one cluster. Large vertical gaps between merges indicate a natural number of clusters.

\section{Regression}

% ---- 5.3.1 Linear & Non-linear Regression -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Linear \& Non-linear Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Linear Regression and the Normal Equation}
    Linear regression fits a linear function $\hat{y} = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$ by minimizing the sum of squared residuals (Ordinary Least Squares)\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~86--90]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. When the design matrix $\mathbf{X}$ has full column rank, the unique minimizer is given in closed form by the normal equation.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \underbrace{\min_{\mathbf{w}} \|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\|^2}_{\text{OLS objective}}
        \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
        \hat{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\mathbf{X}$ is the $n\times d$ design matrix of inputs, $\mathbf{y}$
the vector of targets, and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ the fitted coefficients. See the
caution in \cref{ch:mathematical-foundations} on why this form should not be
implemented literally.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Polynomial and Non-linear Regression}
    Non-linear regression extends the linear model by mapping inputs through basis functions $\phi_j(\mathbf{x})$, e.g.\ polynomial features $[1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^d]$\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~90--97]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The model remains \emph{linear in the parameters} $\mathbf{w}$, so the normal equation still applies. High-degree polynomials can overfit (high variance), motivating regularization.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{y} = \sum_{j=0}^{d} w_j\, x^j = \mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\phi}(x),
        \qquad \boldsymbol{\phi}(x) = [1,\, x,\, x^2,\, \ldots,\, x^d]^\top
    \end{equation}
\where{$d$ is the polynomial degree, $w_j$ the coefficient on the $j$-th
power, and $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)$ the vector of basis functions --- the model is
non-linear in $x$ but still linear in $\mathbf{w}$.}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.3.2 Logistic Regression ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Logistic Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Logistic Regression as a Classification Model}
    Despite its name, logistic regression is a \emph{classification} method. It models the probability of class membership by mapping a linear combination of features through the logistic (sigmoid) function $\sigma$, squashing any real value into the interval $[0, 1]$\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~89--90]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Training maximizes the likelihood (no closed-form solution; gradient descent is used).
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b\right) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left[-\!\left(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b\right)\right]}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\mathbf{w}$ are the feature weights, $b$ the intercept, and $\sigma$
the logistic function that maps the real-valued score to $(0,1)$.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{The Sigmoid Curve and Log-Odds}
    The sigmoid function produces the characteristic S-shaped curve shown below\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.89]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. In log-odds (logit) form, the model is linear: $\log\frac{P(y=1|\mathbf{x})}{1-P(y=1|\mathbf{x})} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$. The logistic loss $\ell^{(\log)}(y,\hat{y}) = \log(1 + \exp[-y\hat{y}])$ is a smooth, convex surrogate for the 0/1 loss.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0]
        \begin{scope}[xscale=0.55, yscale=2.2]
            % axes
            \draw[->] (-5.5,0) -- (5.5,0) node[right, font=\small] {$z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b$};
            \draw[->] (0,-0.08) -- (0,1.15) node[above, font=\small] {$\sigma(z)$};
            % dashed reference lines
            \draw[dashed, gray!60] (-5.5,1) -- (5.5,1);
            \draw[dashed, gray!60] (-5.5,0.5) -- (5.5,0.5);
            % tick marks
            \foreach \x in {-4,-2,0,2,4} {
                \draw (\x,0.03) -- (\x,-0.03);
                \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x,0) {\x};
            }
            \node[left, font=\tiny] at (0,1)   {$1$};
            \node[left, font=\tiny] at (0,0.5) {$0.5$};
            % sigmoid curve
            \draw[thick, blue!70!black, domain=-5:5, samples=80]
                plot (\x, {1/(1+exp(-\x))});
            % decision boundary marker
            \filldraw[red] (0,0.5) circle (2pt);
            \node[right, font=\tiny, text=red] at (0.1, 0.58) {decision boundary};
        \end{scope}
    \end{tikzpicture}
\captionof{figure}{The logistic curve. The model is linear in log-odds and
S-shaped in probability; the decision boundary sits where $z=0$ and
$\sigma(z)=0.5$, but that threshold is a choice, not a property of the model.}
\label{fig:ml-sigmoid}

\begin{workedexample}[Reading logistic coefficients as odds]
A model for loan default has $z = -3.0 + 0.05\,(\text{age}) + 1.2\,
(\text{prior default})$, with prior default coded $0$ or $1$.

\emph{A 40-year-old with no prior default.}
$z = -3.0 + 0.05(40) + 0 = -1.0$, so
\[
  P = \sigma(-1.0) = \frac{1}{1+e^{1.0}} = \frac{1}{3.718} = 0.269 .
\]

\emph{The same person with a prior default.}
$z = -1.0 + 1.2 = +0.2$, so $P = \sigma(0.2) = 1/(1+e^{-0.2}) = 0.550$.

\emph{What the coefficient means.} The probability jumped from $0.269$ to
$0.550$, but the coefficient $1.2$ is not a probability change --- it is a
change in \emph{log-odds}. Exponentiating gives the odds ratio
$e^{1.2} = 3.32$: a prior default multiplies the odds of default by $3.3$,
regardless of age. Odds ratios are constant across the curve; probability
changes are not, which is why the same coefficient moves the probability a lot
near $z=0$ and almost nothing out in the tails.
\end{workedexample}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.3.3 Quantile Regression ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Quantile Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Quantile Regression and the Pinball Loss}
    Quantile regression\textsuperscript{\cite{koenkerRegressionQuantiles1978}} estimates conditional \emph{quantiles} of the response (e.g.\ the median, or the 10th and 90th percentiles) rather than the conditional mean. It is robust to outliers and makes no assumption of normality or homoscedasticity; fitting multiple quantile levels together yields a full prediction interval. The loss function (pinball loss) applies asymmetric linear penalties.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \rho_\tau(u) =
        \begin{cases}
            \tau \cdot u       & u \geq 0 \\
            (\tau - 1)\cdot u  & u < 0
        \end{cases},
        \quad
        \min_{\mathbf{w}} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \rho_\tau\!\left(y_n - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_n\right),
        \quad \tau \in (0,1)
    \end{equation}
\where{$u$ is the residual (actual minus predicted), $\tau\in(0,1)$ the
quantile being estimated, and $N$ the number of examples. The asymmetry is the
point: $\tau=0.9$ penalises under-prediction nine times as heavily as
over-prediction.}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.3.4 Multivariate Regression --------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Multivariate Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Multiple and Multivariate Regression}
    Multiple regression extends linear regression to $D$ input features ($\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1 x_1 + \cdots + w_D x_D$); multivariate regression further allows $K$ response variables simultaneously, with coefficient matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times K}$\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~86--97]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The same normal equation applies. As $D$ grows, multicollinearity can destabilize $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}$, motivating regularization.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W},
        \qquad
        \hat{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{Y}
        \quad (D \text{ features},\; K \text{ responses})
    \end{equation}
\where{$D$ is the number of input features and $K$ the number of responses
predicted simultaneously, so $\mathbf{W}$ is $D\times K$ and each column is one
ordinary regression.}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.3.5 Lasso & Ridge Regression -------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Lasso \& Ridge Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Ridge Regression (L2) and Lasso (L1)}
    Regularized regression adds a penalty on the weights to prevent overfitting\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~91--97]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Ridge (L2) shrinks all coefficients uniformly and has a closed-form solution. Lasso (L1) drives some coefficients \emph{exactly} to zero, performing automatic feature selection; it requires iterative solvers (coordinate descent, ISTA). The hyperparameter $\lambda$ controls the bias-variance trade-off.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \underbrace{\min_{\mathbf{w}} \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2}_{\text{Ridge: } \hat{\mathbf{w}}=(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}}
        \qquad
        \underbrace{\min_{\mathbf{w}} \tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_1}_{\text{Lasso: no closed form}}
        \label{eq:ml-ridge-lasso}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\lambda \geq 0$ sets the penalty strength ($\lambda=0$ recovers plain
least squares), $\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$ is the sum of squared coefficients, and
$\|\mathbf{w}\|_1$ the sum of their absolute values.}
    Ridge is due to Hoerl and
    Kennard\textsuperscript{\cite{hoerlRidgeRegressionBiased1970}} and the lasso
    to Tibshirani\textsuperscript{\cite{tibshiraniRegressionShrinkageSelection1996}}.

\begin{workedexample}[Ridge shrinks, lasso deletes --- in one line of algebra]
Take the smallest possible problem: one feature, $\mathbf{X} = (1,2,3)^{\!\top}$
and $\mathbf{y} = (1,2,3)^{\!\top}$. Then
$\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{X} = 1+4+9 = 14$ and
$\mathbf{X}^{\!\top}\mathbf{y} = 14$, so the unregularised solution is
$\hat w_{\text{OLS}} = 14/14 = 1$.

\emph{Ridge.} $\hat w = \dfrac{14}{14+\lambda}$:
\[
  \lambda = 0 \Rightarrow 1.000,\quad
  \lambda = 7 \Rightarrow 0.667,\quad
  \lambda = 14 \Rightarrow 0.500,\quad
  \lambda = 126 \Rightarrow 0.100 .
\]
It approaches zero but never reaches it, for any finite $\lambda$.

\emph{Lasso.} Setting the derivative to zero for $w>0$ gives
$14w - 14 + \lambda = 0$, so
\[
  \hat w = 1 - \frac{\lambda}{14}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  \lambda = 7 \Rightarrow 0.500,\qquad
  \lambda = 14 \Rightarrow \mathbf{0} \text{ exactly.}
\]

\emph{The difference in one sentence.} Ridge divides, so the coefficient decays
smoothly toward zero; lasso subtracts, so the coefficient hits zero at a finite
penalty and stays there. That is why lasso performs feature selection and ridge
does not --- and it is the algebraic counterpart of the corner geometry in the
figure below.
\end{workedexample}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Elastic Net and the Geometry of Sparsity}
    Elastic Net combines both penalties ($\lambda_1\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 + \lambda_2\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$), inheriting Lasso's sparsity and Ridge's stability under multicollinearity\textsuperscript{\cite{zouRegularizationVariableSelection2005}, \cite[][pp.~91--97]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The geometric picture below explains \emph{why} L1 gives sparse solutions: the OLS contours first touch the L1 diamond at a corner (where a coordinate is zero), whereas L1 ellipsoid contacts are typically at smooth points.

\section{Support Vector Machines}

% ---- 5.4.1 Introduction to SVMs ----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Introduction to Support Vector Machines}

\begin{v3topic}{Maximum Margin Hyperplane}
    A Support Vector Machine\textsuperscript{\cite{cortesSupportVectorNetworks1995}} finds the unique hyperplane $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b = 0$ that maximizes the geometric margin $\gamma = 2/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ between the two classes\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~98--101]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Only the training points closest to the hyperplane — the \emph{support vectors} — determine the solution. The complexity of the learned classifier depends on the number of support vectors, not the dimensionality of the input space.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.05]
        % ---- class A points (circles, top-right) ----
        \foreach \px/\py in {2.8/2.5, 3.5/1.9, 3.1/3.2, 4.0/2.8} {

        }
        % ---- class B points (crosses, bottom-left) ----

            \draw[red!70!black, thick]
                (\px-0.12,\py-0.12) -- (\px+0.12,\py+0.12)
                (\px+0.12,\py-0.12) -- (\px-0.12,\py+0.12);
        }
        % ---- decision hyperplane ----
        \draw[thick, black] (1.0,3.5) -- (3.5,0.3) node[below right, font=\small] {$\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b=0$};
        % ---- margin hyperplanes ----
        \draw[dashed, gray]  (0.4,3.5) -- (2.9,0.3);
        \draw[dashed, gray]  (1.6,3.5) -- (4.1,0.3);

        \draw[<->, thick, gray!80] (0.7,3.5) -- (1.3,3.5)

    \end{tikzpicture}
\captionof{figure}{The maximum-margin hyperplane. Only the circled support

\label{fig:ml-svm-margin}

\begin{plainlanguage}
Many straight lines separate two clouds of points. The SVM picks the one that
leaves the widest empty corridor between them, on the reasoning that a boundary
with room to spare is less likely to be wrong about the next point. Only the
handful of points sitting right on the edge of that corridor matter --- those
are the support vectors, and the rest of your data is irrelevant to the answer.
\end{plainlanguage}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Hard-Margin vs.\ Soft-Margin SVM}
    Hard-margin SVM requires the data to be linearly separable; soft-margin SVM introduces slack variables $\xi_n \geq 0$ to allow misclassifications, controlled by the cost parameter $C$\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~98--101]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Large $C$ penalizes errors heavily (narrow margin, risk of overfitting); small $C$ permits more errors (wider margin, better generalization).
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \min_{\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol{\xi}}\; \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C\sum_{n=1}^{N}\xi_n
        \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
        y_n(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_n + b) \geq 1 - \xi_n,\;\; \xi_n \geq 0 \;\;\forall n
    \end{equation}
\where{$\xi_n \geq 0$ is the slack for example $n$ --- how far it is allowed to
violate the margin --- and $C>0$ prices that violation. Large $C$ makes
violations expensive and narrows the margin.}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.4.2 SVM for Classification --------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{SVM for Classification}

\begin{v3topic}{The Kernel Trick}
    For non-linearly separable data, the kernel trick\textsuperscript{\cite{boserTrainingAlgorithmOptimal1992}} maps inputs into a high-dimensional feature space $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ where a linear separator exists — without ever computing $\phi$ explicitly\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~128--140]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The decision function depends only on inner products, which the kernel $K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \phi(\mathbf{x}_i)\cdot\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)$ computes directly. SVM training always finds a global optimum (convex QP).
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,wd=3.2cm]|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Kernel}      & \textbf{Formula}                                                                                      \\
        \hline
        Linear               & $K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j$                                       \\
        \hline
        Polynomial (degree $h$) & $K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = (\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j + 1)^h$                            \\
        \hline
        Gaussian RBF         & $K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$ \\
        \hline
        Sigmoid              & $K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \tanh(\kappa\,\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}_j - \delta)$              \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Common kernels. Each one implicitly defines a different
feature space; the RBF kernel's space is infinite-dimensional, which the
algorithm never has to represent because it only ever needs inner products.}
\label{tab:ml-kernels}

\begin{workedexample}[The kernel trick, computed both ways]
Take two-dimensional points $\mathbf{x} = (1,2)$ and $\mathbf{z} = (3,1)$, and
the polynomial kernel of degree $h=2$.

\emph{The kernel way.} Two multiplications, one addition, one square:
\[
  \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z} = 1(3) + 2(1) = 5,
  \qquad
  K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) = (5+1)^2 = \mathbf{36}.
\]

\emph{The explicit way.} That kernel corresponds to the six-dimensional
feature map
\[
  \phi(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl(1,\ \sqrt{2}x_1,\ \sqrt{2}x_2,\ x_1^2,\ x_2^2,\
  \sqrt{2}x_1x_2\bigr),
\]
so $\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (1,\ 1.414,\ 2.828,\ 1,\ 4,\ 2.828)$ and
$\phi(\mathbf{z}) = (1,\ 4.243,\ 1.414,\ 9,\ 1,\ 4.243)$. Their inner product:
\[
  1 + 6 + 4 + 9 + 4 + 12 = \mathbf{36}. \checkmark
\]

\emph{Identical answers.} The left-hand computation never constructed the
six-dimensional space; it obtained the same number from the original
two-dimensional coordinates. That is the entire trick.

\emph{And why it matters.} For degree $h$ over $d$ features the explicit map has
$\binom{d+h}{h}$ dimensions --- for $d=100,\ h=3$ that is over $176{,}000$ --- and
for the RBF kernel the corresponding space is \emph{infinite}-dimensional and
cannot be written down at all. The kernel is computable in both cases because
the SVM's decision function only ever needs inner products, never the
coordinates themselves.
\end{workedexample}

\where{$\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j$ are two data points, $h$ the polynomial
degree, $\sigma$ the RBF width (small $\sigma$ makes the kernel see only very
close neighbours), and $\kappa,\delta$ the sigmoid kernel's scale and offset.}

\begin{engineernote}
The RBF kernel is the sensible default for a small tabular dataset, with $C$
and $\gamma$ tuned together by cross-validation --- they interact, so a grid
over both beats tuning them separately. Two practical constraints decide
whether an SVM is viable at all: kernel SVM training scales roughly between
quadratically and cubically in the number of samples, which makes it
impractical much beyond a few tens of thousands of rows, and the kernel matrix
depends on distances, so features \emph{must} be scaled first. An unscaled SVM
is not a weak model; it is a broken one.
\end{engineernote}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Multiclass SVMs}
    SVMs are inherently binary classifiers. Two standard strategies extend them to $K$ classes\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~413--415]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. \emph{One-vs-All} trains $K$ classifiers (class $k$ against all others) and assigns the class with the highest decision score. \emph{One-vs-One} trains $\binom{K}{2}$ binary classifiers and uses majority voting. The decision function for a new point is $d(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}\!\left(\sum_{i} y_i \alpha_i K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}) + b_0\right)$.
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.4.3 SVM for Regression ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{SVM for Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Support Vector Regression (SVR) and the $\varepsilon$-Tube}
    Support Vector Regression applies the maximum-margin principle to regression by defining an $\varepsilon$-insensitive tube around the predicted function\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~98--101]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Residuals smaller than $\varepsilon$ incur zero loss; residuals outside the tube are penalized linearly via slack variables $\xi_n, \xi_n^* \geq 0$. Only points outside the tube become support vectors, yielding sparse solutions.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \min_{\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^*}\; \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\xi_n + \xi_n^*)
        \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
        \left|y_n - \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_n - b\right| \leq \varepsilon + \max(\xi_n, \xi_n^*),\;\; \xi_n,\xi_n^* \geq 0
    \end{equation}
\where{$\varepsilon$ is the half-width of the insensitive tube inside which
residuals cost nothing, and $\xi_n,\xi_n^*$ are the slacks for points falling
above and below it.}
\end{v3topic}

\section{Decision Trees \& Ensemble Methods}

% ---- 5.5.1 Introduction to Decision Trees ------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Introduction to Decision Trees}

\begin{v3topic}{Decision Trees: Structure and Construction}
    A decision tree partitions the feature space recursively: each internal node applies a test on one feature, each branch follows an outcome, and each leaf assigns a prediction\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~8--20]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. The tree is built greedily by choosing at each step the feature split that maximizes a purity criterion --- the approach taken by ID3\textsuperscript{\cite{quinlanInductionDecisionTrees1986}} and by CART\textsuperscript{\cite{breimanClassificationRegressionTrees1984}}. Decision trees are highly interpretable but unstable — small data changes can produce very different trees.

\label{fig:ml-decision-tree}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Splitting Criteria and Pruning}
    The split at each node is chosen to maximize impurity reduction\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~330--344]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. Pre-pruning stops growth early (threshold on gain); post-pruning grows a full tree then removes subtrees that do not improve validation accuracy. Both strategies reduce overfitting.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,wd=2.4cm]|X|Q[l,m,wd=2.6cm]|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Algorithm} & \textbf{Splitting criterion}                                           & \textbf{Split type}   \\
        \hline
        ID3                & Information Gain $= H(D) - \sum_v \tfrac{|D_v|}{|D|}H(D_v)$           & Multi-way             \\
        \hline
        C4.5               & Gain Ratio $= \text{Gain}(A)/\text{SplitInfo}(A)$                     & Multi-way             \\
        \hline
        CART               & Gini Index $= 1 - \sum_k p_k^2$                                       & Binary                \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{The three classical tree-induction algorithms and what
distinguishes them. C4.5's gain ratio exists specifically to stop information
gain from preferring a high-cardinality identifier column.}
\label{tab:ml-tree-algorithms}

\begin{cautionbox}[Information gain favours columns with many distinct values]
A customer-ID column splits a dataset into perfectly pure single-row leaves, so
its information gain is maximal --- and it generalises to nothing at all. This
is not a hypothetical: it is the standard way an ID or timestamp column
silently destroys a tree model. C4.5's gain ratio divides by the split's own
entropy to penalise exactly this, and CART's binary splits limit it
structurally. The reliable fix is still to remove identifier columns before
training.
\end{cautionbox}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.5.2 Decision Trees for Classification ----------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Decision Trees for Classification}

\begin{v3topic}{Entropy, Gini, and Information Gain}
    Classification trees assign the majority class of training examples reaching each leaf\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~330--344]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. Purity at a node is measured by \emph{entropy} (used by ID3/C4.5) or the \emph{Gini index} (used by CART). Information Gain selects the feature that reduces entropy the most. C4.5 normalizes by SplitInfo to avoid bias toward features with many distinct values.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        H(D) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k,
        \qquad
        \text{Gini}(D) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2,
        \qquad
        \text{Gain}(D,A) = H(D) - \sum_{v} \frac{|D_v|}{|D|}\,H(D_v)
        \label{eq:ml-information-gain}
    \end{equation}
\where{$D$ is the set of examples at a node, $p_k$ the fraction belonging to
class $k$, $A$ a candidate splitting feature, and $D_v$ the subset taking value
$v$ on that feature; $|D_v|/|D|$ weights each branch by its size.}

\begin{workedexample}[Choosing a split by information gain]
Fourteen days of records, of which $9$ are ``play'' and $5$ are ``don't play''.

\emph{Step 1 --- entropy before the split.}
\[
  H(D) = -\tfrac{9}{14}\log_2\tfrac{9}{14} - \tfrac{5}{14}\log_2\tfrac{5}{14}
       = 0.6429(0.6374) + 0.3571(1.4854)
       = 0.410 + 0.531 = 0.940 \text{ bits.}
\]

\emph{Step 2 --- split on \textsf{Outlook}} into Sunny ($2$ yes, $3$ no),
Overcast ($4$ yes, $0$ no) and Rain ($3$ yes, $2$ no):
\[
\begin{aligned}
  H(\text{Sunny}) &= -\tfrac{2}{5}\log_2\tfrac{2}{5}
    -\tfrac{3}{5}\log_2\tfrac{3}{5} = 0.529 + 0.442 = 0.971,\\
  H(\text{Overcast}) &= 0 \quad\text{(pure --- every case is ``play'')},\\
  H(\text{Rain}) &= 0.971 \quad\text{(same proportions as Sunny)} .
\end{aligned}
\]

\emph{Step 3 --- weighted entropy after the split.}
\[
  \tfrac{5}{14}(0.971) + \tfrac{4}{14}(0) + \tfrac{5}{14}(0.971)
  = 0.347 + 0 + 0.347 = 0.694 \text{ bits.}
\]

\emph{Step 4 --- gain.}
\[
  \text{Gain}(D,\textsf{Outlook}) = 0.940 - 0.694 = \mathbf{0.247} \text{ bits.}
\]

\emph{Interpret.} Knowing the outlook removes about a quarter of a bit of
uncertainty out of the $0.940$ available. Almost all of that reduction comes
from the Overcast branch, which is perfectly pure and needs no further
splitting --- the other two branches are barely more informative than the
parent. The tree-growing algorithm computes this number for every candidate
feature and takes the largest, then recurses on each impure branch.

\emph{Gini for comparison.} $\text{Gini}(D) = 1 - (9/14)^2 - (5/14)^2
= 1 - 0.413 - 0.128 = 0.459$. Gini and entropy disagree about the best split
only rarely; Gini is the default in CART mainly because it avoids computing a
logarithm.
\end{workedexample}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.5.3 Decision Trees for Regression --------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Decision Trees for Regression}

\begin{v3topic}{Regression Trees: Piecewise-Constant Approximation}
    Regression trees predict the \emph{mean} target value of training examples in each leaf\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~8--20]{iiiCourseMachineLearning}}. Splits are chosen to minimize the weighted sum of within-leaf variances (squared error). The result is a piecewise-constant approximation of the target function. Ensemble methods (Random Forests, Gradient Boosted Trees) dramatically improve regression tree performance on real data.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Split criterion:} \quad
        \min_{A,\, t}\;\sum_{n \in D_{\text{left}}}\!\!(y_n - \bar{y}_{\text{left}})^2
        +\sum_{n \in D_{\text{right}}}\!\!(y_n - \bar{y}_{\text{right}})^2,
        \quad
        \hat{y}_{\text{leaf}} = \frac{1}{|D_{\text{leaf}}|}\sum_{n \in D_{\text{leaf}}} y_n
    \end{equation}
\where{$A$ is the feature and $t$ the threshold defining the split,
$D_{\text{left}}$ and $D_{\text{right}}$ the resulting subsets, and
$\bar{y}$ the mean target within a subset --- so the criterion minimises
within-leaf variance.}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.5.4 Random Forests -----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Random Forests}

\begin{v3topic}{Bagging and the Random Forest Algorithm}
    A Random Forest\textsuperscript{\cite{breimanRandomForests2001}} trains $T$ decision trees on \emph{bootstrap} samples (sampling $N$ points with replacement) and combines their predictions by majority vote (classification) or averaging (regression)\textsuperscript{\cite{breimanBaggingPredictors1996}, \cite[][pp.~586--588]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. At each split, only a random subset of $m \approx \sqrt{D}$ features is considered. This double randomization decorrelates the individual trees and drastically reduces variance while keeping bias low.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} h_t(\mathbf{x}),
        \quad
        \text{bootstrap sample: } \mathcal{D}_t \sim \mathcal{D} \text{ with replacement},
        \quad
        m \approx \sqrt{D} \text{ features per split}
    \end{equation}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{workedexample}[Where the $37\,\%$ comes from]
Drawing $N$ points with replacement, the chance a particular point is
\emph{missed} on one draw is $1-1/N$. Over $N$ independent draws,
\[
  P(\text{never drawn}) = \left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)^{N}
  \xrightarrow{\;N\to\infty\;} e^{-1} = 0.368 .
\]
Numerically: $0.349$ at $N=10$, $0.366$ at $N=100$, $0.368$ at $N=1000$. So
roughly $37\,\%$ of the training set is out-of-bag for each tree, and averaging
each point's prediction over only the trees that never saw it gives a
generalisation estimate for free --- no separate validation split required.
This is the closest thing in classical machine learning to obtaining
cross-validation without paying for it.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{v3topic}{Out-of-Bag Error and Feature Importance}
    Each bootstrap sample leaves out roughly 37\% of the training points (out-of-bag, OOB)\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.588]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. Predicting each training point with the trees that did \emph{not} train on it gives a free, unbiased estimate of generalization error without a separate validation set. Feature importance is measured by how much prediction accuracy drops when a feature's values are randomly permuted (permutation importance).

\begin{cautionbox}[Impurity-based feature importance is misleading]
The default importance attribute in most libraries is mean impurity decrease,
and it is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features for the same
reason information gain is. It also splits credit arbitrarily between
correlated features, so a genuinely important variable can look unimportant
because a correlated twin absorbed its share. Prefer permutation importance
computed on \emph{held-out} data, and treat any importance ranking as a
hypothesis to test rather than an explanation.
\end{cautionbox}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.5.5 Gradient Boosting --------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Gradient Boosting}

\begin{v3topic}{Gradient Boosting: Sequential Error Correction}
    Gradient Boosting\textsuperscript{\cite{friedmanGreedyFunctionApproximation2001}} builds an ensemble sequentially: each new tree $h_t$ fits the \emph{negative gradient} (pseudo-residuals) of the loss of the current ensemble\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.~590]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. For squared-error loss, these pseudo-residuals equal the ordinary residuals $y_n - F_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_n)$. A learning rate $\eta$ (shrinkage) scales each tree's contribution to prevent overfitting.

    \begin{equation}
        F_t(\mathbf{x}) = F_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}) + \eta\, h_t(\mathbf{x}),
        \quad

            \left[ -\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(y_n, F_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_n))}{\partial F_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_n)} - h(\mathbf{x}_n) \right]^2
    \end{equation}
\where{$F_t$ is the ensemble after $t$ rounds, $h_t$ the tree added at round
$t$, and $\eta\in(0,1]$ the learning rate that shrinks each tree's
contribution. The bracketed term is the negative gradient of the loss --- the
pseudo-residual the new tree is fitted to.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{XGBoost and LightGBM}
XGBoost\textsuperscript{\cite{chenXGBoostScalableTree2016}} extends gradient boosting with a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss, $L_1$/$L_2$ regularisation on tree weights, and efficient column subsampling. LightGBM\textsuperscript{\cite{keLightGBMHighlyEfficient2017}} further accelerates training via Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB), enabling learning on datasets with millions of rows. Both consistently rank among the top performers on tabular-data benchmarks\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.~590]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}.

        \hline
        Gradient Boosting & Fit residuals sequentially                               & General tabular regression/classification \\
        \hline
        XGBoost           & 2nd-order loss expansion, L1/L2 reg., column subsampling & Kaggle competitions, finance   \\
        \hline
        LightGBM          & GOSS + EFB, leaf-wise growth                             & Large-scale datasets           \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}

\label{tab:ml-boosting-variants}

\begin{engineernote}
For tabular data, gradient-boosted trees remain the strongest default, and a
tuned LightGBM or XGBoost still beats a neural network on most tabular
benchmarks. Reach for deep learning when the input has structure a tree cannot
exploit --- pixels, audio, text, graphs --- not because it is newer. And when
you report that a boosted model beat a network, state the tuning budget each
one received: an untuned baseline is not a comparison.
\end{engineernote}
\end{v3topic}

\section{Nearest Neighbor Methods}

% ---- 5.6.1 K-Nearest Neighbors -----------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{K-Nearest Neighbors}

\begin{v3topic}{K-Nearest Neighbors Algorithm}
    K-Nearest Neighbors\textsuperscript{\cite{coverNearestNeighborPattern1967}} is a non-parametric, instance-based learner: instead of fitting an explicit model, it stores the training data and at prediction time finds the $k$ closest training examples to the query point\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~396--400]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. For classification, it returns the majority class among the $k$ neighbors; for regression, it returns their mean. There is no training phase, but prediction cost is $O(ND)$ per query without indexing.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x})} y_i
        \quad \text{(regression)}
        \qquad
        \hat{c}(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x})} \mathbf{1}[y_i = c]
        \quad \text{(classification)}
    \end{equation}
\where{$\mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x})$ is the set of the $k$ training points
nearest to the query $\mathbf{x}$, and $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is one when its
condition holds and zero otherwise.}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Weighted Voting and Choosing k}
    Distance-weighted k-NN gives closer neighbors more influence: the weight of neighbor $i$ is $w_i = 1/d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)^2$\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.399]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. The choice of $k$ is critical: $k=1$ is susceptible to noise (high variance); large $k$ smooths the boundary but may blur distinct clusters (high bias). Cross-validation is used to select the optimal $k$.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[c,m]|X|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{$k$ value} & \textbf{Bias}    & \textbf{Variance}  \\
        \hline
        $k = 1$            & Low              & High (noise-sensitive)  \\
        \hline
        Moderate $k$       & Moderate         & Moderate (recommended)  \\
        \hline
        $k = N$            & High             & Very low (predicts overall majority) \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{How $k$ moves the bias--variance balance. The two extremes
are both useless in opposite directions, which is why $k$ is chosen by
cross-validation rather than by rule of thumb.}
\label{tab:ml-knn-k}

\begin{workedexample}[One noisy point decides the answer for small $k$]
Classify the query $\mathbf{q} = (4,4)$ against six labelled points. Compute
every Euclidean distance:

\smallskip
\begin{tblr}{
  width=0.86\linewidth,
  colspec={Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]Q[c]},
  row{1}={font=\bfseries,bg=AlmanachMist},
  hlines,vlines}
point & label & $d$ to $(4,4)$ & rank \\
$(4,3)$ & B & $\sqrt{0+1} = 1.000$ & 1 \\
$(2,3)$ & A & $\sqrt{4+1} = 2.236$ & 2 \\
$(6,6)$ & B & $\sqrt{4+4} = 2.828$ & 3 \\
$(3,1)$ & A & $\sqrt{1+9} = 3.162$ & 4 \\
$(1,2)$ & A & $\sqrt{9+4} = 3.606$ & 5 \\
$(7,7)$ & B & $\sqrt{9+9} = 4.243$ & 6 \\
\end{tblr}
\smallskip

Now vote, for three values of $k$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  k=1:\ &\{\text{B}\} &&\Rightarrow \textbf{B}\\
  k=3:\ &\{\text{B},\text{A},\text{B}\} &&\Rightarrow \textbf{B}\ (2\text{--}1)\\
  k=5:\ &\{\text{B},\text{A},\text{B},\text{A},\text{A}\}
        &&\Rightarrow \textbf{A}\ (3\text{--}2)
\end{aligned}
\]

\emph{What happened.} The point $(4,3)$ is a lone B sitting deep inside A
territory --- a mislabelled or anomalous observation. At $k=1$ and $k=3$ it
dominates the vote purely because it is nearest. At $k=5$ the surrounding A
points outvote it. This is the bias--variance trade-off made concrete: small
$k$ has the flexibility to honour every training point, including the wrong
ones.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{cautionbox}[$k$-NN without feature scaling is measuring the wrong thing]
Euclidean distance adds squared differences across features, so a feature
measured in metres and a feature measured in millimetres contribute at wildly
different weights. Predicting house prices from \texttt{rooms} (range 1--8) and
\texttt{area\_cm2} (range 500{,}000--3{,}000{,}000) means the area term is
larger by a factor of roughly $10^{11}$ --- the room count is arithmetically
invisible, and the model is a one-feature model that nobody intended.
Standardise every feature before computing any distance. The same warning
applies to $k$-means, SVM, and PCA, and to nothing at all in a decision tree,
which is invariant to monotone rescaling.
\end{cautionbox}
\end{v3topic}

% ---- 5.6.2 Distance Metrics -------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Distance Metrics}

\begin{v3topic}{Euclidean, Manhattan, and Minkowski Distances}
    The choice of distance metric strongly affects k-NN behavior\textsuperscript{\cite[][p.397]{hanDataMiningConcepts2012}}. Euclidean distance ($p=2$) is the standard geometric distance but is sensitive to scale; feature normalization is essential. Manhattan distance ($p=1$) is more robust to outliers. For high-dimensional data, all distance metrics suffer from the curse of dimensionality (distances concentrate), and cosine similarity may be preferable.
\visualseparator
    \begin{equation}
        d_{\text{Minkowski}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \left(\sum_{j=1}^{D} |x_j - z_j|^p\right)^{1/p},
        \quad
        \begin{cases}
            p = 1: & \text{Manhattan} \\
            p = 2: & \text{Euclidean} \\
            p \to \infty: & \text{Chebyshev } (\max_j |x_j - z_j|)
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\where{$D$ is the number of features, $x_j,z_j$ the $j$-th component of each
point, and $p\geq1$ the order that selects the metric. All of them require the
features to be on comparable scales first.}
\end{v3topic}

\section{Genetic Algorithms}

% ---- 5.7.1 Introduction to Genetic Algorithms --------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Introduction to Genetic Algorithms}

\begin{v3topic}{Terminology: Chromosomes, Genes, and Fitness}
    A genetic algorithm\textsuperscript{\cite{hollandAdaptationNaturalArtificial1975}} maintains a \emph{population} of candidate solutions called \emph{chromosomes}\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~91--95]{hurbansGrokkingArtificialIntelligence2020}}. Each chromosome is a sequence of \emph{genes} (encoding one attribute each); the value stored in a gene is its \emph{allele}. A \emph{fitness function} assigns a scalar score to each chromosome, quantifying how well it solves the problem. GAs require no gradient information — only the ability to evaluate fitness.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,wd=2.4cm]|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Term}    & \textbf{Meaning}                                                        \\
        \hline
        Chromosome       & A complete candidate solution (e.g.\ a binary string $[1,0,1,1,0,\ldots]$) \\
        \hline
        Gene / Allele    & One position in the chromosome / the value stored there                 \\
        \hline
        Population       & Set of $N$ chromosomes evaluated in each generation                     \\
        \hline
        Fitness function & Maps chromosome $\to$ scalar score (higher = better solution)          \\
        \hline
        Genotype         & Encoded representation; \emph{phenotype} = the actual decoded solution  \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Genetic-algorithm vocabulary, which is borrowed wholesale
from biology. The only part that carries real engineering weight is the
encoding: it decides which solutions are even reachable.}
\label{tab:ml-ga-terminology}

\begin{plainlanguage}
A genetic algorithm is organised trial and error with memory. Guess a few
hundred solutions at random, score them, throw away the worst, and build the
next batch by mixing pieces of the survivors and randomly tweaking a few
values. Repeat. Nothing understands the problem at any point --- but because
good pieces get copied more often than bad ones, the population drifts toward
better solutions. It is the method of last resort, and the method of first
resort when you have no gradient.
\end{plainlanguage}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{The GA Life Cycle}
    A GA iterates through a fixed life cycle until a stopping condition is met (maximum generations, fitness threshold, or stagnation)\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~95--110]{hurbansGrokkingArtificialIntelligence2020}}. Selection pressure drives exploitation of high-fitness regions; mutation and diverse initialization drive exploration. This balance prevents premature convergence to suboptimal solutions.
\visualseparator
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Encode} the solution space (binary, real-valued, permutation, or tree representation)
        \item \textbf{Initialize} a random population of $N$ chromosomes
        \item \textbf{Evaluate} fitness $f_i$ for each chromosome
        \item \textbf{Select} parents proportionally to fitness (roulette wheel: $P_i = f_i / \sum_j f_j$; or tournament)
        \item \textbf{Crossover}: combine two parents to produce offspring (single-point, two-point, or uniform)
        \item \textbf{Mutate}: randomly perturb genes with small probability $p_m$ to maintain diversity
        \item \textbf{Replace} the old population with the new generation (elitism: keep top solutions)
        \item \textbf{Repeat} steps 3--7 until the stopping condition is satisfied
    \end{enumerate}

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Crossover and Mutation Operators}
    Crossover (recombination) exchanges genetic material between two parent chromosomes to produce offspring, exploiting existing good partial solutions\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~100--115]{hurbansGrokkingArtificialIntelligence2020}}. Mutation randomly flips bits or perturbs values, reintroducing diversity lost through selection. The mutation rate $p_m$ is kept small (typically 0.001--0.01) to avoid turning the GA into a random search.
\visualseparator
    \begin{tblr}{width=\linewidth,colspec={|Q[l,m,wd=3.0cm]|X|}}
        \hline
        \textbf{Operator}       & \textbf{Description}                                                                  \\
        \hline
        Single-point crossover  & One cut point; swap the tail segments of two parents                                 \\
        \hline
        Two-point crossover     & Two cut points; swap the middle segment                                              \\
        \hline
        Uniform crossover       & Each gene independently chosen from either parent via a random binary mask            \\
        \hline
        Bit-flip mutation       & Each gene flipped with probability $p_m$ (binary encoding)                           \\
        \hline
        Gaussian mutation       & Gene perturbed by $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ (real-valued encoding)                   \\
        \hline
    \end{tblr}
\captionof{table}{Crossover exploits combinations that already work; mutation
explores combinations nobody has tried. A GA that loses either operator
degenerates --- into a random search without crossover, into a premature
monoculture without mutation.}
\label{tab:ml-ga-operators}

\subsection{Applications of Genetic Algorithms}

\begin{v3topic}{Application Domains and Configurable Parameters}
    Genetic algorithms excel in large, complex, or discontinuous search spaces where gradient methods fail\textsuperscript{\cite[][pp.~115--127]{hurbansGrokkingArtificialIntelligence2020}}. They are robust to noisy fitness functions and require no problem-specific gradient. For example, the Knapsack Problem with 26 items requires $2^{26} \approx 67$ million brute-force evaluations; a GA typically finds near-optimal solutions in 10\,000--100\,000 fitness evaluations. GAs provide no guarantee of global optimality.

    \textbf{Key parameters to configure:} population size $N$, mutation rate

\begin{cautionbox}[A genetic algorithm is the answer only when there is no gradient]
GAs are attractive because they need nothing but a fitness function, and that
same generality is their weakness: they ignore all the structure a gradient
would have given them for free. If your objective is differentiable, gradient
descent will beat a GA by orders of magnitude in evaluations. Reserve genetic
algorithms for genuinely non-differentiable, combinatorial, or black-box
objectives --- and even then, benchmark against random search and simulated
annealing, both of which are simpler and surprisingly often competitive.
\end{cautionbox}

\section{Deep Dive: From Score to Decision Policy}
\begin{v3topic}{Probability, Threshold, Action}
A classifier score becomes useful only through a decision policy.
Choose thresholds from false-positive and false-negative consequences,
capacity constraints, calibration, and an abstention or review path.
Accuracy at the default threshold rarely captures this operating decision.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Calibration and Coverage}
Measure reliability curves and proper scoring rules on held-out data.
When uncertainty is material, compare calibrated probabilities, prediction
intervals, or conformal sets and report empirical coverage and set size by
slice.
Coverage without useful sharpness can still produce an unusable system.

\end{v3topic}
\begin{v3topic}{Data-Centric Error Loop}
After a baseline, cluster errors by mechanism: ambiguous target, label error,
missing feature, distribution gap, shortcut, capacity limit, or threshold
policy.
Make one intervention at a time, preserve a protected evaluation set, and
measure whether improvement transfers beyond the error slice that inspired the
change.
\end{v3topic}

\begin{practicallab}[Build the baseline that makes your model prove itself]
\textbf{Goal.} Establish, on a dataset of your choosing, the number every later
model must beat --- and discover how often that number is embarrassingly hard
to beat.

\textbf{Protocol.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Pick any tabular dataset with a target column. Record its size, class
        balance, and the meaning of one prediction.
  \item \textbf{Split first, before you look at anything.} Hold out a test set
        and do not touch it again until step 7. Split by group or by time if
        either applies.
  \item Compute the three trivial baselines: majority class (classification) or
        mean (regression); a single-feature rule; and a decision tree of
        depth 2. Report all three on cross-validation, with the spread.
  \item Fit a regularised linear or logistic model, then a gradient-boosted
        tree. Tune each with the same budget --- count the trials and write the
        number down.
  \item Report precision, recall, $F_1$, and the majority-class baseline
        together. Never report accuracy alone.
  \item Take the twenty worst errors and read them. Classify each as label
        error, missing feature, genuine ambiguity, or model limitation.
  \item Only now, once, score your chosen model on the test set. Whatever it
        says is your answer.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Deliverable.} One table with every model, its cross-validation mean and
spread, its tuning budget, and the trivial baseline in the first row. Plus one
paragraph on what the twenty errors told you.

\textbf{The point.} Step 6 usually changes the project more than step 4 does.
A model that beats the baseline by less than the fold-to-fold spread has not
been shown to beat it at all.
\end{practicallab}
